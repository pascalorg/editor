## Completion at a glance

**~72% of line items, and a wall the takeoff prices correctly and nobody can build is now reported** — see the caveats below, which matter more than the number. The clash pass that just landed is the second of phase 9's six, and it closes a gap of a kind worth naming: two corner units claiming one stretch of face produced a bill in which *every figure was self-consistent*. `panelRuns` subtracts each blocked stretch in turn, so an overlap leaves a shorter panel run rather than an open one — no `unfilledMm`, no `UNFORMABLE_STRIP`, and two corner units billed for a return with room for one. Nothing in the product disagreed with anything else, which is why it needed an invariant rather than a fix.

Counting partials as half: **27 done, 9 partial, 8 not started → 72%**, and the invariant suite is now reachable from all three surfaces rather than from code alone. The solver pipeline scores separately at **5 of 13 phases done, 7 partial → 65%**.

The per-phase percentages above are **not** all strict row arithmetic, and the two places they diverge are deliberate. P7 reads 46% against a row count of 17%, because its one partial row carries 17 invariants and two clash passes and counting that as half a row of three understates it by more than the rounding hides. P8 reads 26% against 38%, on tool count across the two surfaces rather than on its four rows — the undo row is a whole row and a small fraction of the surface, so here the row count is the one that flatters. Everywhere else the number is `(done + partial/2) / rows`.

**Three reasons that count overstates progress.** Recorded because the percentage will otherwise be read as a schedule.

1. **The remaining phases are the expensive ones.** P5 (guillotine placer with SA over piece order) and P7's clash passes are each larger than several completed phases combined, and P8 is ~14 further chat tools plus ~23 more on MCP. Line-item counting treated "CSV export" and "cut optimisation" as one row each; the first has now closed in a session and the second will not. Ties × openings and panels × intersections were the two cheapest of the six clash passes precisely because both of their inputs were already solved; the ones behind them (ties × rebar, props × slab-below) each need geometry the scene does not carry.
2. **The `solve.ts` that exists is not the plan's.** `nodes/src/formwork-assembly/solve.ts` solves one *host* — every shutter on a wall, with its parts — and it is what the panel and the chat tool share. The plan's central abstraction is wider: `(scene, settings, catalog) → FormworkSolution` across the project, which phase 12 emits and most of P4/P7/P8 consume. The per-host solve is the honest half of it: it fixed the divergence hazard between the screen and the AI, and it did not produce a project-level solution type. `solve-project.ts` has since put a project *scope* on top of it — `solveProjectFormwork` walks the castable elements in a level or a whole scene and merges their bills into one — so a rollup is now askable. But it is an aggregation over per-host solves, not the plan's `FormworkSolution`: it carries takeoff and, now, nothing else — `validateFormwork` is a separate pass over the scene rather than a field on the solution, so a set count and a crane schedule still have nothing to read.
3. **Validation is now reachable, and five of its assertions still cannot run at all.** The Buildability panel and `validate_formwork` both sweep a scope, so a user and the model can now be told a bill is for something nobody can erect. What they are told is bounded: five of the plan's assertions are *permanently* unavailable here and say so in `notChecked` (rebar geometry, crane curves, the slab below's capacity, a per-system minimum radius, the set count from phase 10) — two of them still because phase 9's remaining clash passes do not exist. Four more are conditional on a solved layout, so an element nobody has shuttered is honestly reported as unexamined rather than as passing. A shutter can therefore still clash in fact and read clean, and the panel says which checks it did not make rather than leaving the reader to assume it made them all.

**The fair characterisation: the engine is roughly two-thirds there, it audits itself, and the audit now reaches the user.** Everything that *computes* — pressure, the beam core, both design chains, coverage, layout, measurement, the parts, and the invariants — is largely done and tested. What is still missing on the presenting side is cost and the cut sheet: no price, no owned/hired split, no offcut plan. Nine things have now landed and they compound: the design report made the engine legible, the settings panel made it governable, the parts model made it *addressable* — the walers the report describes at 300 mm centres are nine objects with marks, each of which can be clicked, priced, substituted or left off the order — reconciliation made it *durable*, the takeoff made it *orderable* at the scope a yard actually delivers to, the invariant suite made it *answerable*, and the Buildability panel and `validate_formwork` make that answer *readable*: the claim that a bill is for something nobody can build is now in front of the person about to place the order. The clash pass is the first check whose subject is what the model *chose not to draw* — every invariant before it read something the shutter produced. And the two answers are now *portable*: they are askable by an agent that has never opened the editor, over the same modules, in the same words. The ninth is of a different sort and belongs at the end of that list rather than in it: an AI edit is now *reversible*, which is the thing that makes the other eight safe to let an agent touch at all.

## What landed: the engine can say a bill is for something nobody can build

Every formwork module before this answered "what does this need". `validateFormwork(nodes, options)` answers "is what it needs buildable", and the two have almost no overlap in what makes them wrong. A wall billed at 138 parts with every figure individually correct can still have a lift joint through a window, a skew corner no hinged unit sweeps, and a monolithic pour that has to be cast both before and after its neighbour.

**The substance of the work was auditing the suite against the real code, and four checks failed that audit.** Each one had been written to a plausible reading of the plan, each type-checked, and each could not fail against any scene — which is worse than no check, because it reports a pass. They are recorded in the file at the check they replaced:

- **`CAST_ORDER_CYCLE` had to be a graph over pours, not elements.** `castOrder` is one integer per element, so an edge between two elements always runs from a lower number to a higher one and the element graph is acyclic by construction. `pourId` is what makes a cycle possible: elements sharing one contract to a single node, and their individual orders become claims about that whole node. Two walls in pour P either side of a wall in pour Q say P must be cast before Q *and* after it — a pour somebody cannot place, and neither wall's own order is wrong.
- **`AREA_DOUBLE_COUNTED` was a tautology.** The first version recovered the gross area by adding the deductions back onto the net, which is the same arithmetic that produced the net. The real invariant is *ownership*: `ownsOverlap` guarantees exactly one of a pair deducts a junction prism, and both deducting is a stretch nobody forms and nobody bills. Deliberately **not** asserted: the case where *neither* side deducts, because a junction can legitimately be nobody's surface — a monolithic corner is one pour and the overlap is interior.
- **`EXPANSION_JOINT_BRIDGED` could not fire**, because a joint's own hard cut becomes the segment boundary the check was looking for. What actually bridges one is a shared `pourId` across it, or a cut dropped by `MIN_SEGMENT_LENGTH` (1e-3) for sitting too near an end — right for geometry, wrong for a joint somebody mis-entered.
- **`POUR_VOLUME_OVER_SUPPLY` could not fire for walls**, which are the one kind `maxPourVolume` already splits. It fires for slabs — `pourUnitsForElement` returns a slab as one pour by construction, since bay-splitting a polygon is unwritten — and columns, whose volume comes from plan area. The messages are kind-specific because the fix differs: a slab needs a bay joint, a column needs a bigger delivery.

**Two decisions in the report's shape.** `severity` means buildability, not confidence: `error` is "cannot be built as specified", `warning` is "buildable by an exception somebody accepts". A severity meaning "how sure are we" produces a list sorted by the author's mood. And `notChecked` is part of the report rather than an omission, because a report listing only failures reads as a clean bill of health for everything it never examined — five assertions are permanently unavailable here (rebar geometry, crane curves, the slab below's capacity, a per-system minimum radius, phase 10's set count) and four more are conditional on the caller passing the packs, the envelopes, the system or the tie fields.

Findings carry `elementIds` plural, because the interesting failures are relational: a cycle names its whole ring, a bridged joint both sides. A finding naming only the first would send the reader to a wall that looks fine on its own.

`designEnvelope` moved to `core/systems/formwork/design/envelope.ts` in the same change, deleting the copy in `nodes/formwork-assembly/geometry-shared.ts`. It is the single place pressure inputs are assembled, and a second copy is how the wall chain and the validator come to design to different pours.

## What landed: the check a user reads, and the evidence it runs on

The engine could say a bill was for something nobody can erect, and nothing in the product said it. Two surfaces now do — the **Buildability** host panel beside the takeoff, and `validate_formwork` — and the interesting part of the work was not either of them. It was that three of the fourteen invariants had nowhere to read from.

`UNFORMABLE_STRIP` is a property of a packed run, `DESIGN_OUTSIDE_CODE_ENVELOPE` of a pressure solve, `WALL_OUTSIDE_TIE_RANGE` of a catalog system. None of the three survive into the node graph — `unfilledMm` is gone from the pieces the moment `planFace` substitutes a cut board for a run it cannot fill — so a validator handed only nodes reported them as `notChecked` and always would.

**The validator could have re-derived them, and that is the one thing it must not do.** Packing every face a second time inside the check produces a second layout, and two layouts of one wall disagree about which run has the open strip the first time either changes. That is the same hazard `parts.ts` was written against one layer down (the bill and the 3D shutter out of one enumeration) and `solve.ts` one layer up (the panel and the AI off one solve). So the shutters are built once and the evidence comes *out* of that pass: `PartCollector.evidence()` and `ShutterEvidence` on every `SolvedShutter`, which `validate-project.ts` turns into the three maps `validateFormwork` takes.

**Deciding what counts as an element's evidence is where the reasoning is**, and each of these was a way to report one fault as several or none:

- **One skin, not both.** Panel joints align up the wall by strip-pack rule 3, so both faces are packed identically — carrying both would report every open strip twice.
- **The base course, not every course.** For the same reason: the courses above are the same division at another height, and reporting each would multiply one open strip by the stack.
- **Every shutter, not the first.** A 9 m wall in three lifts is three packs, and the open strip may be in any of them.
- **The base lift's envelope is the element's worst**, because pressure grows with the head above the point. A lift higher up cannot be outside an envelope the base lift is inside.
- **A slab contributes neither, correctly.** A deck is loaded by weight rather than head, so it has no envelope, and it is not packed from a run. Both checks stay `notChecked` for it rather than passing it.

**`ValidateOptions.system` was singular and wrong**, caught while wiring this. `systemId` is a field on the *assembly*, so one wall on a job can be Framax and the next TRIO; a scope-wide system checks wall B's thickness against wall A's catalog, which is a check against hardware that is not on the wall. It is now a per-element map, and two tests pin it — a 250 mm wall is outside every TRIO tie's range and inside Framax's, so the old design faulted the wrong one. A corner unit spans *both* walls of a junction, so where the two differ every candidate is tried and the corner is only faulted when none sweeps it: reporting a bespoke corner because the first system cannot turn it would send a carpenter to a junction the yard can close.

`ValidateOptions` also grew `elementIds`, because a selection had no way to scope the findings. An empty array is an empty scope rather than an absent one — asked about nothing, the report says nothing was checked instead of reporting the whole scene.

**Both surfaces show what was not checked, always.** The panel gives it its own section below the findings and names how many elements in scope had no shutter to examine; the tool returns `notChecked` and `shutteredIds`. This is the one way a validation surface can be worse than none: a report listing only failures reads as a clean bill of health for everything it never looked at, and "no clashes found" from a pass that never had rebar geometry is the most misleading sentence this feature could print. For the same reason errors and warnings are separated rather than sorted together — a single severity-ordered list still reads as one worklist, and the reader has to find the boundary themselves.

Every finding row selects its `elementIds`, and there is one button for every failing element off `failingElementIds`. A validator naming `wall_3` in a project with forty walls has told the reader almost nothing they can act on; the id is only useful as a way of getting to the wall.

Covered by `formwork-assembly/validate-project.test.ts` (16) and `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts` (12). The nodes suite is about the wiring rather than the checks — that the four layout invariants actually stop being `notChecked` once an element is shuttered, that a wall is checked against its own catalog, that a slab and a column contribute what they have and nothing more. The chat suite asserts the model's `summary` is `===` the panel's, so the two cannot drift into two phrasings of one fault.

## What landed: a bill at the scope a yard delivers to

Every formwork readout before this was per element, and that is not a scope anybody orders at. A yard delivers to a floor. The gap was not a missing button — it was that the two bills a user could already read *could not be added together*: the same panel type on two walls is one line on a delivery note, so summing two per-element takeoffs produces a plausible order that no yard can pick. The aggregation had to happen before the numbers were shown, not in the reader's head.

`solveProjectFormwork(nodes, scope)` is that aggregation, and the decision worth recording is that it is built **on** `solveShuttersForHost` rather than beside it. A project-scope enumeration of its own would have been easier and would have reintroduced, one level up, exactly the screen-versus-AI divergence `solve.ts` was written to prevent. It is proved by a test that omits a part on one wall through `set_formwork_part` and asserts the project weight falls: an independent enumeration would quietly re-order what somebody had struck off.

Three surfaces read it, and they share their words as well as their numbers:

- **The takeoff panel** (project or level), a host panel because a scope is not a node anybody can select — the same reasoning as the settings panel it sits beside.
- **The CSV**, at both scopes, through one `bomCsv` — so the button on a wall and the button on a floor are one export at two scopes rather than two exports.
- **`inspect_project_formwork`**, the parity half. It is the first tool that could answer "what does this floor need" at all.

All three print `projectFormworkCaveats(solution)` verbatim, because three phrasings of one fault is how a user comes to believe two of them are different problems.

**Two things a project total hides, and both are now stated.** An element with no shutter at all is *omitted* from a bill of what exists, and an empty row reads as "needs nothing" — so unshuttered elements are named separately, in the panel as `N short` and in the tool as an `unshuttered` array. It is the likeliest reason a total is lower than a user expects. And an element cast in three pours but formed for one bills a third of what the job needs, with every figure in that third individually correct; `coversWholePour` is carried per element so the caveat can name the wall rather than the scope.

**Two smaller decisions that only look like details.** Rows are ordered by element id, not by node-map iteration, because an undo reshuffles insertion order and a CSV whose rows move between two downloads of an unchanged scene is a CSV nobody can diff — asserted by building the same scene forwards and backwards. And an unknown weight is a blank cell with `incomplete — some parts have no published weight` on the TOTAL row, never a zero: a fabricated zero in a figure somebody might crane-check is worse than an admitted gap.

`downloadBlob` was extracted from `export-manager.tsx` to `packages/editor/src/lib/download.ts` rather than copied, since a second copy is a second place for its missing `revokeObjectURL` to come back.

## What landed: shutters reconcile against the pour

This was queued as "re-solve the stale assemblies after a settings change", and the investigation found that premise half wrong and two real bugs behind it. Both were invisible on screen, both produced perfectly reasonable-looking numbers, and both were reachable by following the application's own instructions.

**A settings change needed no re-solve at all.** Verified empirically before anything was written: change the rate of rise and the worst utilisation on a slab moves from 0.972 to 0.471 on the next read. The parts and the design report both solve *from* the settings each time they are asked, so the new pour is already in every figure. What the code actually did was worse than nothing — `set_formwork_settings` told the model to call `attach_formwork` again, which was unnecessary *and*, once shutters carried per-part decisions, destructive. That instruction is gone; the reply now says what the change reached and that there is nothing to regenerate.

**The real gap was `set_pour_limits`, which changes how many shutters an element needs.** A 9 m wall shuttered as one pour and then capped at 3 m lifts is cast in three and formed for one. Measured on a probe: 3 pour units, 1 assembly, 138 parts' worth of formwork billed as 50. Nothing on screen said so, and the panel's button — guarded on `hasFormwork` since P0 — was *disabled* at exactly that moment.

**And the chat tool appended.** `attach_formwork` called twice took a wall from 1 assembly to 2, `partCount` 50 → 100, weight 2530 → 4945 kg, 50 duplicate marks, and an omitted part appearing twice with `[true, false]` — a part somebody had marked "already on site" quietly re-ordered on the copy. The editor button had been guarded against this for months; the tool never was, and `set_formwork_settings` was instructing the model to do it.

`reconcileFormworkNodes` (`nodes/src/formwork-assembly/attach.ts`) is the shared answer, and both surfaces route through it. A pour unit's identity is `segmentIndex:liftIndex`, not the node id, and the three outcomes are asymmetrical on purpose:

- **keep** — the pour unit still exists, so the node does too, id and `partOverrides` untouched. This is the whole reason it reconciles rather than rebuilding: a shutter is where a person keeps decisions, and a new node is an erased set of them.
- **create** — genuinely new pour units only.
- **orphan** — returned rather than deleted, because deleting them deletes recorded work. `attach_formwork` counts the overrides that die with them and tells the model to say so; the panel warns before the click.

Duplicates already in a scene heal to one, so this repairs graphs the un-guarded append left behind. Between two shutters on one pour unit the survivor is the one carrying more overrides — the edited one holds information. And re-running it when nothing has moved is a no-op, or the routine that repairs a scene is the routine that corrupts it.

On the reading side, `inspect_formwork_parts` now carries `coversWholeElement` and `coverageCaveat`, and the system prompt says to lead with the caveat rather than the bill. This is the one field that invalidates every figure above it: a takeoff for four of an element's twelve pours is not a small error, and each of its numbers is individually correct.

**One pre-existing bug the tests found on the way.** `duplicateMarks` was checked flat across an element's shutters, but a mark encodes station and elevation *within its own pour unit* — so the same panel in two lifts of one wall carries the same mark by design. A correctly shuttered three-lift wall reported every panel it had as a clash. Now checked per shutter, the way the parts panel always did it, and each entry names its assembly. A clash list that fills up whenever an element is split is a clash list nobody reads.

Covered by 9 reconciliation tests in `formwork-assembly/attach.test.ts` (3 mutation-verified) and 15 in `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai-formwork-reattach.test.ts` (4 mutation-verified — appending instead of reconciling fails 9 of them). The `chat-ai-formwork-settings.test.ts` assertion that the reply asks for a re-attach was inverted, since it was pinning the destructive behaviour.

## What landed: the tie that was never drawn

The first of phase 9's clash passes, and the whole of the work was working out that **the clash is not the finding**. `passable()` in `geometry-wall.ts` already refuses to place a tie whose station lands inside an opening, and it is right to: a rod through a void bears on nothing. So an invariant asserting "a tie hits a window" would report a condition the model never produces — it would fire on every wall with a door, and the fix it named would already be applied.

The reportable defect is what refusing leaves behind. A band of concrete beside an opening — a pier between a jamb and a wall end, a head band over a door — can contain **no drilled hole at all**, while being loaded on both faces at full pressure. On a panel system there is nothing to add there, because the frames arrive drilled: the answer is a strut through the box-out or a strongback spanning the opening. That makes it a **warning** rather than an error, since `severity` here means buildability and this is buildable by a detail somebody signs.

**The evidence had to be recorded before it was filtered**, which is the inverse of every other piece of `ShutterEvidence`. A tie never drawn leaves no part, so the emitted ties can never answer the question — `TieField.holes` is the two skins' drilled grid intersected and *unfiltered* by openings, because the openings are the validator's own input and removing them here would hide exactly the loss the check asserts about. Filtering it would be a check that always passes, which this file already records four instances of.

**Three bounds on a tie field, each of which would otherwise report a fault that isn't there.** It is one field per *panelled stretch*, not one per element: a pour unit's stations say nothing about the stretch the next unit forms, and merging them calls a band tied by a hole in a different pour. A corner unit interrupts the panel run and ties through its own holes on the catalog's spacing, so the stretch under it is excluded rather than reported as untied. And both faces' corner runs are used, so every stretch is one the panels form on *both* skins — a rod needs a hole at each end.

**Four judgements in the check itself**, all of them about not producing noise:

- **Rows need a tolerance** (`ROW_TOLERANCE_M = 0.02`), not exact equality. Two runs on one face can be packed with different panel widths and a narrow panel is drilled at its own levels, so the same physical row arrives as two elevations a millimetre apart. Split, each half carries only some of the row's stations and a band tied by the other half reads as untied.
- **`MIN_TIED_BAND_MM = 300` is a site fact, not a tolerance.** A nib that narrow is strutted form to form, which is what a carpenter does with a 200 mm pier; reporting it would put a finding on every wall whose opening lands near its end.
- **One finding per band, not per row.** A full-height opening fails its pier at every course, and four findings about one 800 mm band reads as four problems. The rows are counted in the message and the *lowest* is the locus, because pressure is worst at the base.
- **Only the rows the opening crosses are cut.** A door head at 2.1 m takes nothing out of the row at 2.7 m, and treating every opening as a full-height cut is how a wall with a low window gets reported as untied at the top.

A band bounded only by the shutter's own ends is left to `tieGrid`'s `untied-stretch` — a different fault with a different fix, and reported here too it would be one problem in two lists. Conventional shutters are exempt (`holes.length === 0`): the carpenter bores the ply where the calculation asks, so there is no fixed grid to fall between.

**The check is a function of the layout, not of the opening**, which is the property that makes it worth having and is pinned in all three suites. The same 6 m wall with the same 4.8 m window is faulted in 2.70 m panels — drilled at 1.35 and 4.65 m, so every station is in the void and both piers are untied — and silent in 0.6 m panels, which bring a hole every 300 mm. A check keyed on the opening alone would fault both, and a user who narrowed the panels to fix it would watch the finding stay.

Covered by 12 tests in `validate/invariants.test.ts` keyed on the catalog's real hole positions rather than invented ones (so a catalog change is a test failure, not a check that quietly stops describing the wall), 4 in `validate-project.test.ts` including the field asserted to *keep* the stations an opening blocks, and 2 in `chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts`. Neither surface changed: `FindingRow` already renders a multi-id finding as "2 elements" for `OPENING_STRADDLES_LIFT_JOINT`, and `notChecked` renders generically off `INVARIANT_LABELS`.

## What landed: the questions, askable from outside the editor

The claim in the last section of this file was that the port was thin over shared modules and the work was the tool surface. That held — no engine function changed — but the shape of it was not where the difficulty was. `packages/mcp` runs under Bun with no DOM, and the two functions it needed were behind `formwork-assembly/index.ts`, which reaches React, `@pascal-app/viewer`, `@pascal-app/editor`, `lucide-react` and `zustand`, because the panels live there too.

**The solve itself is headless already, and that was worth establishing empirically rather than assuming either way.** The geometry builders construct `Group`/`Mesh`/`BoxGeometry`, and the part collector reads marks off those meshes — which is the whole reason the bill and the on-screen shutter cannot count differently. `three` builds all of it without a browser and the meshes are then discarded. Measured through the barrel the import cost ~1026 ms; through the pure modules, ~81 ms. So the answer was not `export_glb`'s answer (decline in headless mode), because the solve demonstrably runs and produces the editor's own numbers.

`formwork-assembly/headless.ts` and the `@pascal-app/nodes/formwork-assembly/headless` subpath export are that narrow door: `solveProjectFormwork`, `validateProjectFormwork` and their types, reaching `@pascal-app/core/{formwork,registry,schema}` and `three` and nothing else. **It is a re-export, not a second implementation** — a headless *copy* of the solve is the same divergence hazard `parts.ts` and `solve.ts` were each written against, one layer further out.

**The narrowness is enforced, not documented.** `headless.test.ts` walks the import graph from the entry point over the *source* — so it fails in the editor the moment the import is typed, rather than after a build on someone else's machine — and fails if the chain reaches any package outside that permitted set. A companion test asserts the barrel *does* reach React, so a reviewer reading only `headless.ts` cannot conclude the distinction is decorative. The failure this guards is silent by construction: add `import { FormworkBom } from './parts-summary'` to `solve.ts` and every existing test still passes while the MCP install grows a UI stack.

**The entry point was right and under-used, which cost a broken production build.** This section framed the barrel's weight as an MCP cold-start problem. It is also a hard error one layer over: a Next Route Handler *is* a React Server Component, so a client hook anywhere in its import graph fails the compile rather than merely costing milliseconds. `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai.ts` imported `castableHostIds` and six siblings from the barrel, and `buildSolverJointNodes` from `construction-joint/index.ts`, whose node definition carries the parametric editors — so `editor#build` failed with 55 copies of one error, an import trace running the chat route through the validation panel into every tool in `@pascal-app/editor`. Nothing else showed it. `bun run check-types` and `bun test` both import React without complaint, so the tree carried a build that could not ship while every gate read green.

Fixed by widening the door rather than by adding a second one: `pourUnitsForHost`, `reconcileFormworkNodes` and `solveShuttersForHost` were already headless — `attach.ts` and `solve.ts` reach `@pascal-app/core/*` and `three` and nothing else — so they are now re-exported from `formwork-assembly/headless.ts`, and `construction-joint/headless.ts` is the same narrow door onto `buildSolverJointNodes`. `apps/editor/lib/server-imports.test.ts` walks every `app/api/**/route.ts` and fails on a client-only barrel, naming the entry point to use instead; a companion assertion checks the chat route still reaches the solve, so the guard cannot be satisfied by a route that has stopped doing the work. `bun run build` is now recorded in `AGENTS.md` as a fourth gate alongside types, tests and lint, because it is the only one that sees this class of fault.

**Two deliberate divergences from the chat tools, both MCP idiom rather than new behaviour.** A bad level id is refused with `isError: true` and no `structuredContent` — legal even with an `outputSchema` declared, because the SDK's `validateToolOutput` returns early on an error result — and the refusal says `Call list_levels and read the id of the level you mean.`, since MCP has no `list_castable_elements` to point at. Everything else is shared verbatim: `validationSummary` and `projectFormworkCaveats` come from core, and the suite asserts the tool's `summary` is `===` the panel's, so the server, the panels and the chat cannot drift into a third phrasing of one fault.

**The agent guide is where the system prompt would have gone.** MCP hosts give a server no system prompt, so the reasoning the chat surface states in `SYSTEM_PROMPT` — that formwork is a different model from the building, that neither answer is derivable from `get_scene`, that the two severity counts must never be merged, that `notChecked` and `caveats` are always reported — lives in `pascal://agent-guide` and in the tool descriptions instead. Without it a model holding a scene graph will try to infer a takeoff from wall areas, which is the one failure mode this whole surface exists to prevent.

Verified end to end against the compiled stdio binary rather than only the registrar: 9 BOM lines and 2433.8 kg on a probe wall, the `OPENING_LEAVES_TIE_GAP` finding with its strut advice, five `notChecked` entries, and `levelId: 'level_9'` refused. The probe also caught the one thing a unit test would not have: `create_wall` does not set `formworkType`, so an unformed wall bills nothing — which is exactly the "reads as a floor that needs nothing" case `unshuttered` was added for, arriving unprompted through the tool's own front door.

## What landed: an AI edit the user can take back

The row said "chat mutations bypass undo" and prescribed `runAsSingleSceneHistoryStep`. Both halves were off, and the way they were off is the reason this is recorded rather than just fixed.

**The defect was destruction, not omission.** An agent's result reaches the client over SSE and was applied with `applySceneGraphToEditor`, which is the *load* path and correctly ends in `clearSceneHistory()` — its own comment explains why, and the reason is sound: loading is itself a tracked write, so without the reset a few Ctrl+Z presses step past the load into the previous project, or into an empty scene, and take the whole thing with them. Used for an AI edit, that same reset threw away the history the edit should have been appended to. So the user pressed Ctrl+Z after an AI edit, nothing happened, and their own work from before it was already gone. **There is no error state in that** — an empty history is indistinguishable from a history with nothing left to undo, which is why a defect that loses work outright sat behind a row reading like a missing nicety.

**The prescription pointed at a layer that cannot run it.** `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai.ts` mutates a plain JSON graph, not the store, and the route explains why: core's zustand store is `'use client'`, which Next's Route Handler bundler turns into a client-reference stub the moment it is actually touched server-side. There is no store on the server to wrap a transaction around. The fix has to be where a store exists — the client listener — and the fix is a *second* apply path rather than a change to the first, because clearing history is right for a load and wrong for an edit, and one function cannot be both.

`applyAgentSceneGraphToEditor` is that path, and it takes **every** event the live listener accepts. The first draft gated it on the event's `kind` against an allowlist of `ai_chat` and `ai_construction_plan`, which was wrong twice over. It missed ~21 kinds: MCP's mutation tools each publish under their own tool name — `create_wall`, `apply_patch`, `delete_node`, `furnish_room` and the rest through `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` — and every one of those is equally an agent editing the scene the user is watching. And it was unnecessary, because the listener already discards anything that is not *this* scene at a *higher* version, so nothing arriving on that channel is a load. The three genuine loads — mount, project switch, version preview — are all inside `<Editor>` and still clear history. An allowlist would have quietly withheld undo from the next tool anyone added, which is the same class of silent failure as the original bug.

**Collapsing the step, not merely tracking it, and the distinction is testable.** A populated graph is a single tracked `set` inside `setScene`, so tracking alone looks correct on every ordinary edit — and zundo's `equality: areSceneSnapshotsEqual` already drops the no-op case, so a read-only turn costs nothing either way. The collapse earns its keep on exactly one path: an **empty** graph takes `clearScene()` + `setInstalledPlugins()`, two tracked writes, and two entries mean the first Ctrl+Z appears to do nothing, so the user presses again and loses an edit they wanted. That is the one assertion that distinguishes the shipped behaviour from the plausible-but-wrong version of it.

Covered by `packages/editor/src/lib/agent-scene-history.test.ts` (6), and the suite was verified against two mutations rather than one, because the two failures are different. Reverting to the load path fails **all six**. Tracking without collapsing fails exactly **one** — the empty-graph test — which is the point of having it: without that case the suite would pass on an implementation that costs two presses for one action. Two of the six also needed strengthening for the same reason the original defect was invisible: asserting `pastStates` is empty after a read-only turn passes just as well when history has been wiped, so that test now puts an entry on the stack first and asserts it *survives*.

Nothing about the formwork engine changed, and no formwork test moved. It is in this file because every formwork mutation the AI can make — `attach_formwork`, `set_formwork_part`, `set_pour_limits`, both settings writers — travelled this path, and a tool that reshuffles a wall's shutters is a poor thing to be unable to undo. It also removes a reason not to ship the MCP formwork mutations: the "live-snapshot publish" they were deferred behind now lands as an undoable step for free, since `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` goes down the same listener.

The doc row that prescribed the fix is worth keeping in mind as a pattern: it named a real function and a real problem, and following it literally would have produced a server-side change that cannot compile against a client store, for a defect one order of magnitude worse than the row described. The row was written from the symptom.

## What landed: two corner units where there is room for one

Phase 9's second clash pass, and the mirror image of the first. Ties × openings had to be reported as the *loss* rather than the collision, because the layout already refuses to draw a rod into a void. Here the layout does not refuse anything, and that is the whole defect.

**The takeoff was internally consistent and wrong.** A wall between two returns carries a corner unit at each end, and each leg eats inward from its own junction. `panelRuns` (`geometry-wall.ts`) subtracts each `corner.blocked` stretch in turn and drops runs under `PANEL_GAP`, so two legs claiming the same concrete produce a *shorter panel run*, not an open one. No `unfilledMm`, therefore no `UNFORMABLE_STRIP`; a bill listing two corner units for a return with room for one; and every figure agreeing with every other. Nothing in the product contradicted anything, which is exactly why this needed an invariant and not a fix — there was no inconsistency for a stricter check elsewhere to catch. On a 200 mm wall in Framax the inside legs are clear at 1.0 m and overlap from about 0.79 m down; the outside legs, longer by the core, are already unformable at 0.7 m.

**Two findings, because the fault has two shapes.** `CORNER_UNITS_OVERLAP` is the geometry: a warning normally, since notching one unit back or forming the return as a single bespoke box is a decision rather than a layout, and an **error** where the legs need more of the face than the face has, because then there is no stretch between them to notch back to. `OPENING_INSIDE_CORNER_UNIT` is the plan's "opening too near a corner → custom": a jamb *inside* a leg's extent, where there is no panel joint to move and the box-out has to be cut into a framed unit. A warning, because which answer applies turns on whether the plant is owned or hired and no field records that.

**Reported per face, and the two faces genuinely disagree.** An outside leg wraps the core, so it is longer than its inside counterpart by the wall thickness — 500 mm against 300 on a 200 mm wall. A 700 mm link is therefore two different problems at once: face `a` needs 600 mm of a 700 mm face and its units can be notched, face `b` needs 1000 and cannot. One finding averaged over both would name a stretch that is on neither skin, which is why a single wall can legitimately produce one error and one warning.

**The leg placement moved into core rather than being derived twice.** `cornerLegExtent` in `coverage/junctions.ts` is now the one answer to where a leg lands, read by both `geometry-shared.ts`'s `cornerRuns` (which places the unit) and the validator's `cornerLegsByFace` (which asks whether two collide). The caller still resolves the leg *length*, because only it knows the system — the catalog's fitted leg where a unit sweeps the angle, the derived length where none does and the corner is cut on site. Computed separately, a check and the thing it checks drift, which is the hazard `parts.ts` was written against one layer down. The placement has two subtleties that are wrong in opposite directions if either is dropped: the leg runs toward the element's end or back toward its start, and it starts at the corner of the *concrete* rather than the centreline — which is what makes an outside leg's first joint land at the same station as its inside counterpart's, so a tie between the skins passes through square.

**Three judgements, each pinned by a test that fails without it.** Legs that meet *exactly* are the intended geometry on a return twice the leg length, so the check needs a tolerance — `LEG_OVERLAP_TOLERANCE_M = 0.002`, matching the width at which the layout already calls two panel edges one joint; without it a wall dimensioned 799 mm instead of 800 is a finding. Unformed corners are skipped, because `formed` is monolithic-only and a sequenced junction gets a bulkhead rather than a unit, so checking the legs a monolithic pour *would* have had reports hardware nobody sets. And the opening check tests strict containment rather than a comfort distance: an opening 50 mm off a corner unit is the ordinary case and forms with a filler, so a proximity rule would fault walls the crew builds daily.

**Neither pair needs a shuttered layout, unlike the tie gap, and so neither ever appears in `notChecked`.** Both shipped systems turn a right angle on the same 300 mm leg — PERI's TE 270-2 is 180 × 300, Framax's corners are 300 — so `DEFAULT_INSIDE_CORNER_LEG_M` is the figure they agree on rather than a guess, and a scene that has never opened the formwork settings is told its 500 mm return is unformable just the same. Listing these as unchecked would send the reader off to form something before an answer that is already available.

Covered by 14 tests in `validate/invariants.test.ts`, keyed on the catalog's real leg lengths so a catalog change is a test failure rather than a check that quietly stops describing the wall; 2 in `validate-project.test.ts` (through the solve, and asserting the pair is absent from `notChecked`); 1 in `formwork.test.ts`; and 2 in `chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts`. Six mutations were tried and each killed a different test: dropping the unformable escalation, removing the overlap tolerance, swapping strict containment for a 300 mm comfort distance, naming only the wall instead of its neighbours, ignoring the face split, and skipping the `formed` check. Neither validation surface changed — `FindingRow` already renders a multi-id finding and `notChecked` renders generically off `INVARIANT_LABELS`.

## Shortest path to the next visible win

**Ties × waterstops**, of phase 9's four remaining clash passes, is the only one left in the cheap class. Both halves are already in the graph: a waterstop is a `treatment` on a `ConstructionJointNode`, which `WATERSTOP_RUN_NOT_CLOSED` already reads, and the joint carries the station it sits at — `elevation` for a lift joint, `along` for a pour break — while the drilled holes are in `ShutterEvidence.tieFields`. A tie through a PVC bulb is a hole in the seal, so the check is two lists of stations compared, like the two passes that landed. Ties × rebar, props × slab-below and scaffold × boundary are not in that class: each needs geometry no node carries, which is why they sit in the permanent `notChecked` list rather than the remaining-work one.

Behind it, **the owned/hired split** is the smallest remaining P4 row and the one a costing pass needs first: `bomLines` already groups on provenance, so the question is a schema home for what the yard owns, not new engine work.

Then **the rest of the MCP formwork surface**, now that the door is open: the settings pair, the parts pair, and `set_pour_limits`. The read tools needed only `getNodes()`; the mutations write through the store and want the live-snapshot publish — and that is now a smaller decision than it was, because `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` already reaches an undoable client apply (see the undo section above), so what remains is five thin tools over `mergeFormworkSettingsGroup` and the parts solve.

**Deliberately not built: `solutionCache`.** The plan's schema (§5.1) carries `solutionCache: { hash, solvedAt }` on the assembly, "persisted so a saved scene can produce a BOM without a full re-solve". It is not implemented and it should not be until something actually reads it. Nothing does: the panels solve on demand and the chat tools solve on the server, both in under a frame for a wall. A persisted hash that no code compares is a field that can only be wrong — it would be written by whatever last saved and believed by nothing, which is the precise failure the parts model was built to avoid on the other side. When an export path needs a BOM without the geometry pass, that is the moment to add it, with the reader and the invalidation in the same change.
