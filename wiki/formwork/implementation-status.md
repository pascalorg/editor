## Completion at a glance

**~78% of line items, and the takeoff now knows the difference between a date somebody typed and a date somebody agreed to** — see the caveats below, which matter more than the number. The figure did not move this session, which is the third session running, and the reason is the same arithmetic as last time: commit windows are the last item of *one pipeline phase* rather than a line item of their own, so the phase closes and the row count cannot. What landed is that last item, and it is the first field in this feature whose subject is a conversation rather than a building. Every date before it was stated by whoever typed it and every move was a proposal; `commitments.ts` records that somebody has **accepted** one — this much of this item is spoken for from here to here — and the three disciplines it turns on are all about not overstating that. A commitment is stored as a **date** (`committedPourAt`, the day agreed) and never as a flag, because a boolean would silently follow `pourAt` and re-attach a hire desk's booking to a day nobody rang them about. A committed quantity is **swept over the committed pours alone**, so it comes out under the set count's peak on almost every job, and every surface says it is what has been promised rather than what the job needs and prints it last so a reader cannot take the difference for a shortfall. And moving a committed pour is **allowed and reported** rather than refused: sites move booked pours, and blocking the edit would only push somebody into clearing the commitment first and losing the one record that says the plant is still reserved for the old day. What that closes is the loop the resequencing proposals opened last session — a proposal can now be *taken*, so the takeoff stops re-proposing a move somebody has already agreed not to make, and says so by name rather than by omission.
The session before this one answered the sentence every money figure in this feature had carried since the rates shipped — *"there is no labour in this, and labour is normally the largest of the things outside it"* — rather than repeating it on a fifth surface. `labour.ts` multiplies the project's **own** output norms over the same bill the money is counted over, splits erecting from striking because they are different operations weeks apart, and reports the fittings no norm covers so a total over half a bill reads as a floor. Three disciplines carry it: the hours sit **beside** `cost` and never inside it, because a hire desk and a gang are two negotiations that move for different reasons; they are **man-hours and not a duration**, since no gang size exists anywhere in this model; and there is **no shipped norm table at all**, because an output is a fact about a crew rather than about a product or a code.
The session before this one added the first thing the takeoff *argues*; the sequence and the proposal are described below and unchanged. A shortage used to end at "you are 3 panels short by 1 March, and hiring costs £21 against £630" — a fork with two priced tines and a third the module named and could not evaluate: move a pour out of the overlap. `sequence.ts` and `resequence.ts` close that third one. Precedence comes out of the scene the project has already stated three ways (`liftIndex`, `castOrder`, `pourId`) with no new schema, float is measured against the neighbours' **stated** dates rather than a forward pass nobody agreed to, and the answer to "can this be resequenced to need fewer sets" is now a named pour, a signed number of days, the peak the move leaves behind, and every other catalog id the move makes worse. The two words that discipline all of it are **pinned** and **proposal**: a pour with no allowance is pinned by the dates around it, which is a weaker claim than critical, and a move is an argument to take to a planner, never a plan — there is no gang, no batching plant and no concrete supply in this model.
The bill has answered *what* since the rollup, *whose* since the supply split, *how long* since the striking tables, *how much* since the rates, *when* since the programme, *how many* since the set count, and **what is missing** and **whether to own it** since the acquisition. It now answers the one question none of those touched, because every one of them is about the plant and this one is about the people: **how much work is in it**. That answer is deliberately not in the same total as the rest — see "What landed: the gang's time, and why there is no table behind it" below. A shortfall is the peak against the yard's rack, never the bill against the rack — those two differ by the reuse factor, so a job whose pours run in sequence can carry a large hired quantity in its bill and have nothing at all to acquire. And the buy-or-hire answer is not the trade's rule of thumb: hire is charged per unit per month, so uses do not enter the comparison and only the span held does, which means hiring wins on almost any single job and owning is justified by an order book this model cannot see. So the figure to read is `paybackJobs` — how many jobs like this one a purchase pays back over — rather than the verdict beside it. The same change stopped pretending the yard's own rack is free: owned stock is charged at the project's own hire rate for the period held, as a plant department recharges its own site, and kept out of `totalCost` because it is not cash leaving the business.

Labour was one of four items inside a row already counted as half; commit windows are not a plan row at all — they are the last remaining item of *solver phase 10*, which the plan tracks separately. So the figure that moved is the pipeline's: **6 of 13 phases done, 7 partial → 73%**, up from 69%, and phase 10 is the first phase to close since the layout. That is the honest shape of it — a phase went from partial to done and the line-item table cannot show it, which is the third consecutive session where the headline number understates what shipped and the second where the understatement is structural rather than a rounding.

The per-phase percentages above are **not** all strict row arithmetic, and the two places they diverge are deliberate. P7 reads 72% against a row count of 33%, because its two partial rows carry 19 invariants, three clash passes, the hire cost, the owned-stock recharge and now the gang's hours, and counting each as half a row of three understates them by more than the rounding hides. The 6 points it gained last session are the one place in this document where a per-phase figure moved without a row moving, and the reason is stated above: two of one row's four items now landed where one did. **P6 is the mirror of that this session and did not move at all**: it reads 75%, which is exactly its row arithmetic, and commit windows are not one of its four rows — they are the last item of solver phase 10, which is the figure that moved instead. A phase closing without a row closing is the shape of the whole session. P8 reads 52% against 38%, on tool count across the two surfaces rather than on its four rows — 13 on each now that `commit_pour` has landed on both. That inequality flipped when the surface closed: the row count used to be the flattering one, because the undo row is a whole row and a small fraction of the surface — now the tool count is, because the five MCP tools that closed the surface were the *thin* ones, three of them a few lines over modules the chat surface had already proved. `commit_pour` is the thinnest yet on both surfaces and the least like the others in kind, which is the clearest case this document has that a tool count measures the wrong thing: it is a dozen lines over core's own patch and it is the only write in the feature whose input describes nothing in the model. What the number cannot show is the thing that actually changed, which is not a count: the two surfaces now answer with the same fields, the same refusals and the same sentences, so parity is no longer a thing to keep checking. Everywhere else the number is `(done + partial/2) / rows`.

**Three reasons that count overstates progress.** Recorded because the percentage will otherwise be read as a schedule.

1. **The remaining phases are the expensive ones.** P5 (guillotine placer with SA over piece order) and P7's clash passes are each larger than several completed phases combined, and P8 is ~13 further tools on *each* surface — the MCP deficit closed against the editor's set, not against the plan's, so both stop at the same twelve. Line-item counting treated "CSV export" and "cut optimisation" as one row each; the first has now closed in a session and the second will not. Ties × openings and panels × intersections were the two cheapest of the six clash passes precisely because both of their inputs were already solved; the ones behind them (ties × rebar, props × slab-below) each need geometry the scene does not carry.
2. **The `solve.ts` that exists is not the plan's.** `nodes/src/formwork-assembly/solve.ts` solves one *host* — every shutter on a wall, with its parts — and it is what the panel and the chat tool share. The plan's central abstraction is wider: `(scene, settings, catalog) → FormworkSolution` across the project, which phase 12 emits and most of P4/P7/P8 consume. The per-host solve is the honest half of it: it fixed the divergence hazard between the screen and the AI, and it did not produce a project-level solution type. `solve-project.ts` has since put a project *scope* on top of it — `solveProjectFormwork` walks the castable elements in a level or a whole scene and merges their bills into one — so a rollup is now askable. But it is an aggregation over per-host solves, not the plan's `FormworkSolution`: it carries takeoff, a programme read off stated dates, and now a precedence graph and a resequencing proposal over that programme. `validateFormwork` is still a separate pass over the scene rather than a field on the solution, and the whole thing is still an aggregation rather than a type phases 9–11 write into. The sequence closed one of the two gaps this item had carried since it was written: the programme is no longer only dates, so a set count has something to act on — a crane schedule still does not, because it needs gang picks and a lift weight rather than an order.
3. **Validation is now reachable, and four of its assertions still cannot run at all.** The Buildability panel and `validate_formwork` both sweep a scope, so a user and the model can now be told a bill is for something nobody can erect. What they are told is bounded: four of the plan's assertions are *permanently* unavailable here and say so in `notChecked` (rebar geometry, crane curves, the slab below's capacity, a per-system minimum radius) — two of them because phase 9's remaining clash passes do not exist. The fifth left that list this session: `SET_COUNT_SHORTAGE` had narrowed its reason twice and has now fired, so it is conditional rather than permanent — unavailable while nobody has dated a pour or recorded a rack, and answered the moment both exist. Five more are conditional on a solved layout, so an element nobody has shuttered is honestly reported as unexamined rather than as passing. A shutter can therefore still clash in fact and read clean, and the panel says which checks it did not make rather than leaving the reader to assume it made them all.

**The fair characterisation: the engine is roughly two-thirds there, it audits itself, the audit reaches the user, the bill says what to go out and get, it argues for the cheaper alternative to getting it, it says how much work is in it as well as how much money — and it now knows which of its own dates somebody has actually agreed to.** Everything that *computes* — pressure, the beam core, both design chains, coverage, layout, measurement, the parts, and the invariants — is largely done and tested. What is still missing on the presenting side is the cut sheet and part of a cost: no offcut plan, and no transport, finance or amortisation. Labour has left that list, with one condition attached that is not a caveat but the design: the hours are the *project's* norms multiplied over this bill, so a project that has stated none gets none, and every surface says so rather than showing zero. The hire cost closed the third of three factors — the bill knows *whose* rack each line comes off, *how long* each line is held, and what the project pays to hold it — the programme closed a fourth question of a different kind, *when*, the set count closed a fifth that none of the four was the scope for, *how many stand at once*, and the acquisition has now closed the two that follow from the whole chain: *what is short*, and *whether to own it*. And the sequence has now closed the one that followed from *those two together*, which neither could answer alone: *what to do about it other than pay*. And the fourteenth closed the one that followed from *all* of them and is not about the plant at all: *how much work is in it*. And the fifteenth has now closed the one that is not a question about the job but about its own answers: *which of these dates has anybody agreed to*. What none of that has done is make the feature able to quote a job or to plan one, and the second half of that sentence has moved this session. Planning is still blocked on the three constraints outside this model, and that is unchanged — but the gap between a proposal and a programme is narrower than it was, because a proposal can now be accepted and the takeoff stops arguing with a decision somebody has taken. Quoting is no longer blocked on labour: the hours exist, they are priced where a gang rate does, and `cost.excludes` now names the sibling block rather than claiming there is none. What quoting is blocked on instead is narrower and more honest — a project that has stated no norms has no hours, transport and finance are still absent, and neither has a quantity anywhere in this model to hang off. Planning is blocked differently: precedence, float and a resequencing proposal exist, so the gap is no longer that the model cannot say what depends on what — it is that formwork precedence is one constraint among four, and the other three (gangs, batching plant, concrete supply) are not in this model at all. A move that is feasible here can be impossible on site for reasons nothing here can see, which is why every surface calls a move a proposal and none of them calls it a plan. Fifteen things have now landed and they compound: the design report made the engine legible, the settings panel made it governable, the parts model made it *addressable* — the walers the report describes at 300 mm centres are nine objects with marks, each of which can be clicked, priced, substituted or left off the order — reconciliation made it *durable*, the takeoff made it *orderable* at the scope a yard actually delivers to, the invariant suite made it *answerable*, and the Buildability panel and `validate_formwork` make that answer *readable*: the claim that a bill is for something nobody can build is now in front of the person about to place the order. The clash pass is the first check whose subject is what the model *chose not to draw* — every invariant before it read something the shutter produced. The whole feature is also *portable*: not just the two answers but every question behind them is askable by an agent that has never opened the editor, over the same modules, in the same words. An outside agent can find the castable elements, state how each is built and how it is cast, raise the shutters, read the parts and the bill, record the yard's decisions about them, date the pours, and check that any of it can be erected — the same thirteen calls the editor's own AI has, so the feature has one behaviour rather than a good version and an MCP version of it. The tenth answered a question about the calendar rather than about the concrete: the bill became *bookable*, because a delivery date is a thing somebody can put in a diary and a strike date is a thing a following trade waits on. The eleventh is the one a yard's money turns on: the bill became *purchasable*, because what a job passes through and what somebody has to own at once are different numbers, and only the second is a decision. The twelfth was the first that is an *instruction* rather than a figure: the bill became *actionable*, because "you are 3 panels short and they have to be on site by 1 March, and hiring them costs £21 against £630 to buy" is a thing somebody does on a Monday morning, where a peak beside a rack is a thing somebody still has to work out. The thirteenth is the first that is neither a figure nor an instruction but an *argument*: the bill is now *negotiable*, because "push the level-2 pour eight days and you need 60 panels instead of 84, at the cost of two more props in the same week" is a thing somebody takes into a programme meeting, where a shortfall and a hire price is a thing somebody takes to a supplier. The fourteenth is the first whose subject is the *crew* rather than the kit: the bill is now *resourceable*, because "there are 640 man-hours of erecting and striking in this, and 180 fittings in it carry no norm at all" is a thing a contracts manager staffs and prices against, where a hire figure is a thing a plant department pays. The fifteenth finished this session and is the only one whose subject is not the job at all but a *conversation about* the job: the bill is now *bookable in the strong sense* — not "here is a date somebody could put in a diary", which the tenth gave, but "this much of this item is spoken for from the 9th to the 23rd, and the pour behind it is not one this takeoff will offer to move". It is the first thing here that distinguishes a figure the project intends from a figure somebody has promised, and it is what makes the thirteenth safe to act on: a proposal that can be accepted is worth more than one that gets re-proposed on every read. One of the fifteen is of a different sort and belongs at the end of the list rather than in it: an AI edit is *reversible*, which is what makes the other fourteen safe to let an agent touch at all.

The other trap in these fixtures is that a steel-panel wall bills ties and walers as well as panels and shortfalls sort by size, so a rack holding only panels leaves the hardware genuinely short and `answers[0]` may be a tie — the rack has to be derived from the scope's own peaks.

**The commitment suite's lesson is that a gate can hide the one case a module exists for**, and it took a failing MCP test to find rather than any of the 13 unit tests under it. `core/systems/formwork/commitments.test.ts` (13) covers the sweep and the drift: a window over the committed pours alone coming out under the same scope's peak, a signed `driftDays` in both directions, a cleared date reported with no `pourAt` at all rather than as a drift of zero, and `undefined` where nothing is committed. `resequence.test.ts` grew 7 for the exclusion, `schedule-patch.test.ts` 4 for the write (11 in the file), and the four scope surfaces 4 in the CSV, 4 in the aggregation, 7 in the MCP suite and 7 in the chat one. The MCP test for a *cleared* date is the one that mattered: it asserted the caveat saying the plant is reserved for a pour the programme no longer places, and found nothing, because `solveProjectFormwork` was gating `commitments` on `anyDated` alongside the schedule, the sets, the acquisition and the sequence. Every one of those is right to vanish when the last date on a job is cleared and this one is not — a booking outlives the date it was made against, and gating it meant the single state nothing else in the feature would report was the state that disappeared. The unit tests could not have caught it: they call `formworkCommitments` directly, which is exactly the module that was behaving correctly.

**Two of this session's failures were test bugs of a kind already recorded here, and both recur.** A CSV assertion quoted `"no date — cleared"` on the reasoning that a cell with punctuation is escaped; `cell()` quotes on a comma, a quote, a newline or surrounding space and an em-dash is none of those, so the field is emitted bare — the same class of mistake as the `row.split(',')` lesson below, one level out. And a chat-surface test compared a pour id from one built scene against a solve of a second: each `build()` call raises its own shutters and ids are generated, so the free and the booked runs share none. The fixture now checks each run against its own second pour, which is the shape the file's other multi-solve tests already use.

Three of the CSV assertions are worth recording as a lesson rather than as coverage, because each was a *test* bug that the implementation was right about, and all three recur. A `row.split(',')` against a cell containing a comma reads `"longest` as a field, so an assertion over the striking columns has to be a `toContain` on the quoted form. The file rounds to 2 dp, so a test cannot assert the raw `hours / 24`. And `bomHire`/`bomSupply` key their maps on the `BomLine` *object*, so a test that builds one array for the producer and a different one for the serialiser gets empty cells and reads as a wiring failure.

The wall chain's geometry tests pin the two things a constant cannot do: the grid tightens on its own with the lift height (a 2.4 m lift gets three rows at 300 mm centres, a 4 m lift six rows at 250 mm), and a drilled system's ties land on the factory grid with the walers following them rather than the solved spacing. Both were verified against the builder's actual output rather than asserted from the arithmetic.

**APA Example 2 now reproduces**: 412 psf bending against the published 412. Shear reads 647 psf and deflection 387 psf against the published 714 and 370 — APA deducts support width from the clear span where this solves on centre-to-centre, so the two bracket the published pair rather than matching it. Recorded here because the gap is a definition, not an error, and someone will otherwise "fix" it.

Still the gap: the DIN calculator probes, CIRIA's 53.86, the HKSMM4 opening cases, the ACI metric/imperial *must-disagree* test, and the property test *`sum(trimmed) === trueWrappedArea` over random wall networks*.

## What landed: the engine can say a bill is for something nobody can build

Every formwork module before this answered "what does this need". `validateFormwork(nodes, options)` answers "is what it needs buildable", and the two have almost no overlap in what makes them wrong. A wall billed at 138 parts with every figure individually correct can still have a lift joint through a window, a skew corner no hinged unit sweeps, and a monolithic pour that has to be cast both before and after its neighbour.

**The substance of the work was auditing the suite against the real code, and four checks failed that audit.** Each one had been written to a plausible reading of the plan, each type-checked, and each could not fail against any scene — which is worse than no check, because it reports a pass. They are recorded in the file at the check they replaced:

- **`CAST_ORDER_CYCLE` had to be a graph over pours, not elements.** `castOrder` is one integer per element, so an edge between two elements always runs from a lower number to a higher one and the element graph is acyclic by construction. `pourId` is what makes a cycle possible: elements sharing one contract to a single node, and their individual orders become claims about that whole node. Two walls in pour P either side of a wall in pour Q say P must be cast before Q *and* after it — a pour somebody cannot place, and neither wall's own order is wrong.
- **`AREA_DOUBLE_COUNTED` was a tautology.** The first version recovered the gross area by adding the deductions back onto the net, which is the same arithmetic that produced the net. The real invariant is *ownership*: `ownsOverlap` guarantees exactly one of a pair deducts a junction prism, and both deducting is a stretch nobody forms and nobody bills. Deliberately **not** asserted: the case where *neither* side deducts, because a junction can legitimately be nobody's surface — a monolithic corner is one pour and the overlap is interior.
- **`EXPANSION_JOINT_BRIDGED` could not fire**, because a joint's own hard cut becomes the segment boundary the check was looking for. What actually bridges one is a shared `pourId` across it, or a cut dropped by `MIN_SEGMENT_LENGTH` (1e-3) for sitting too near an end — right for geometry, wrong for a joint somebody mis-entered.
- **`POUR_VOLUME_OVER_SUPPLY` could not fire for walls**, which are the one kind `maxPourVolume` already splits. It fires for slabs — `pourUnitsForElement` returns a slab as one pour by construction, since bay-splitting a polygon is unwritten — and columns, whose volume comes from plan area. The messages are kind-specific because the fix differs: a slab needs a bay joint, a column needs a bigger delivery.

**Two decisions in the report's shape.** `severity` means buildability, not confidence: `error` is "cannot be built as specified", `warning` is "buildable by an exception somebody accepts". A severity meaning "how sure are we" produces a list sorted by the author's mood. And `notChecked` is part of the report rather than an omission, because a report listing only failures reads as a clean bill of health for everything it never examined — four assertions are permanently unavailable here (rebar geometry, crane curves, the slab below's capacity, a per-system minimum radius) and five more are conditional on the caller passing the packs, the envelopes, the system, the tie fields or the acquisition.

Findings carry `elementIds` plural, because the interesting failures are relational: a cycle names its whole ring, a bridged joint both sides. A finding naming only the first would send the reader to a wall that looks fine on its own.

`designEnvelope` moved to `core/systems/formwork/design/envelope.ts` in the same change, deleting the copy in `nodes/formwork-assembly/geometry-shared.ts`. It is the single place pressure inputs are assembled, and a second copy is how the wall chain and the validator come to design to different pours.

## What landed: the check a user reads, and the evidence it runs on

The engine could say a bill was for something nobody can erect, and nothing in the product said it. Two surfaces now do — the **Buildability** host panel beside the takeoff, and `validate_formwork` — and the interesting part of the work was not either of them. It was that three of the fourteen invariants had nowhere to read from.

`UNFORMABLE_STRIP` is a property of a packed run, `DESIGN_OUTSIDE_CODE_ENVELOPE` of a pressure solve, `WALL_OUTSIDE_TIE_RANGE` of a catalog system. None of the three survive into the node graph — `unfilledMm` is gone from the pieces the moment `planFace` substitutes a cut board for a run it cannot fill — so a validator handed only nodes reported them as `notChecked` and always would.

**The validator could have re-derived them, and that is the one thing it must not do.** Packing every face a second time inside the check produces a second layout, and two layouts of one wall disagree about which run has the open strip the first time either changes. That is the same hazard `parts.ts` was written against one layer down (the bill and the 3D shutter out of one enumeration) and `solve.ts` one layer up (the panel and the AI off one solve). So the shutters are built once and the evidence comes *out* of that pass: `PartCollector.evidence()` and `ShutterEvidence` on every `SolvedShutter`, which `validate-project.ts` turns into the three maps `validateFormwork` takes.

**Deciding what counts as an element's evidence is where the reasoning is**, and each of these was a way to report one fault as several or none:

- **One skin, not both.** Panel joints align up the wall by strip-pack rule 3, so both faces are packed identically — carrying both would report every open strip twice.
- **The base course, not every course.** For the same reason: the courses above are the same division at another height, and reporting each would multiply one open strip by the stack.
- **Every shutter, not the first.** A 9 m wall in three lifts is three packs, and the open strip may be in any of them.
- **The base lift's envelope is the element's worst**, because pressure grows with the head above the point. A lift higher up cannot be outside an envelope the base lift is inside.
- **A slab contributes neither, correctly.** A deck is loaded by weight rather than head, so it has no envelope, and it is not packed from a run. Both checks stay `notChecked` for it rather than passing it.

**`ValidateOptions.system` was singular and wrong**, caught while wiring this. `systemId` is a field on the *assembly*, so one wall on a job can be Framax and the next TRIO; a scope-wide system checks wall B's thickness against wall A's catalog, which is a check against hardware that is not on the wall. It is now a per-element map, and two tests pin it — a 250 mm wall is outside every TRIO tie's range and inside Framax's, so the old design faulted the wrong one. A corner unit spans *both* walls of a junction, so where the two differ every candidate is tried and the corner is only faulted when none sweeps it: reporting a bespoke corner because the first system cannot turn it would send a carpenter to a junction the yard can close.

`ValidateOptions` also grew `elementIds`, because a selection had no way to scope the findings. An empty array is an empty scope rather than an absent one — asked about nothing, the report says nothing was checked instead of reporting the whole scene.

**Both surfaces show what was not checked, always.** The panel gives it its own section below the findings and names how many elements in scope had no shutter to examine; the tool returns `notChecked` and `shutteredIds`. This is the one way a validation surface can be worse than none: a report listing only failures reads as a clean bill of health for everything it never looked at, and "no clashes found" from a pass that never had rebar geometry is the most misleading sentence this feature could print. For the same reason errors and warnings are separated rather than sorted together — a single severity-ordered list still reads as one worklist, and the reader has to find the boundary themselves.

Every finding row selects its `elementIds`, and there is one button for every failing element off `failingElementIds`. A validator naming `wall_3` in a project with forty walls has told the reader almost nothing they can act on; the id is only useful as a way of getting to the wall.

Covered by `formwork-assembly/validate-project.test.ts` (20 at the time, 26 now) and `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts` (15). The nodes suite is about the wiring rather than the checks — that the layout invariants actually stop being `notChecked` once an element is shuttered, that a wall is checked against its own catalog, that a slab and a column contribute what they have and nothing more. The chat suite asserts the model's `summary` is `===` the panel's, so the two cannot drift into two phrasings of one fault.

## What landed: a bill at the scope a yard delivers to

Every formwork readout before this was per element, and that is not a scope anybody orders at. A yard delivers to a floor. The gap was not a missing button — it was that the two bills a user could already read *could not be added together*: the same panel type on two walls is one line on a delivery note, so summing two per-element takeoffs produces a plausible order that no yard can pick. The aggregation had to happen before the numbers were shown, not in the reader's head.

`solveProjectFormwork(nodes, scope)` is that aggregation, and the decision worth recording is that it is built **on** `solveShuttersForHost` rather than beside it. A project-scope enumeration of its own would have been easier and would have reintroduced, one level up, exactly the screen-versus-AI divergence `solve.ts` was written to prevent. It is proved by a test that omits a part on one wall through `set_formwork_part` and asserts the project weight falls: an independent enumeration would quietly re-order what somebody had struck off.

Three surfaces read it, and they share their words as well as their numbers:

- **The takeoff panel** (project or level), a host panel because a scope is not a node anybody can select — the same reasoning as the settings panel it sits beside.
- **The CSV**, at both scopes, through one `bomCsv` — so the button on a wall and the button on a floor are one export at two scopes rather than two exports.
- **`inspect_project_formwork`**, the parity half. It is the first tool that could answer "what does this floor need" at all.

All three print `projectFormworkCaveats(solution)` verbatim, because three phrasings of one fault is how a user comes to believe two of them are different problems.

**Two things a project total hides, and both are now stated.** An element with no shutter at all is *omitted* from a bill of what exists, and an empty row reads as "needs nothing" — so unshuttered elements are named separately, in the panel as `N short` and in the tool as an `unshuttered` array. It is the likeliest reason a total is lower than a user expects. And an element cast in three pours but formed for one bills a third of what the job needs, with every figure in that third individually correct; `coversWholePour` is carried per element so the caveat can name the wall rather than the scope.

**Two smaller decisions that only look like details.** Rows are ordered by element id, not by node-map iteration, because an undo reshuffles insertion order and a CSV whose rows move between two downloads of an unchanged scene is a CSV nobody can diff — asserted by building the same scene forwards and backwards. And an unknown weight is a blank cell with `incomplete — some parts have no published weight` on the TOTAL row, never a zero: a fabricated zero in a figure somebody might crane-check is worse than an admitted gap.

`downloadBlob` was extracted from `export-manager.tsx` to `packages/editor/src/lib/download.ts` rather than copied, since a second copy is a second place for its missing `revokeObjectURL` to come back.

## What landed: shutters reconcile against the pour

This was queued as "re-solve the stale assemblies after a settings change", and the investigation found that premise half wrong and two real bugs behind it. Both were invisible on screen, both produced perfectly reasonable-looking numbers, and both were reachable by following the application's own instructions.

**A settings change needed no re-solve at all.** Verified empirically before anything was written: change the rate of rise and the worst utilisation on a slab moves from 0.972 to 0.471 on the next read. The parts and the design report both solve *from* the settings each time they are asked, so the new pour is already in every figure. What the code actually did was worse than nothing — `set_formwork_settings` told the model to call `attach_formwork` again, which was unnecessary *and*, once shutters carried per-part decisions, destructive. That instruction is gone; the reply now says what the change reached and that there is nothing to regenerate.

**The real gap was `set_pour_limits`, which changes how many shutters an element needs.** A 9 m wall shuttered as one pour and then capped at 3 m lifts is cast in three and formed for one. Measured on a probe: 3 pour units, 1 assembly, 138 parts' worth of formwork billed as 50. Nothing on screen said so, and the panel's button — guarded on `hasFormwork` since P0 — was *disabled* at exactly that moment.

**And the chat tool appended.** `attach_formwork` called twice took a wall from 1 assembly to 2, `partCount` 50 → 100, weight 2530 → 4945 kg, 50 duplicate marks, and an omitted part appearing twice with `[true, false]` — a part somebody had marked "already on site" quietly re-ordered on the copy. The editor button had been guarded against this for months; the tool never was, and `set_formwork_settings` was instructing the model to do it.

`reconcileFormworkNodes` (`nodes/src/formwork-assembly/attach.ts`) is the shared answer, and both surfaces route through it. A pour unit's identity is `segmentIndex:liftIndex`, not the node id, and the three outcomes are asymmetrical on purpose:

- **keep** — the pour unit still exists, so the node does too, id and `partOverrides` untouched. This is the whole reason it reconciles rather than rebuilding: a shutter is where a person keeps decisions, and a new node is an erased set of them.
- **create** — genuinely new pour units only.
- **orphan** — returned rather than deleted, because deleting them deletes recorded work. `attach_formwork` counts the overrides that die with them and tells the model to say so; the panel warns before the click.

Duplicates already in a scene heal to one, so this repairs graphs the un-guarded append left behind. Between two shutters on one pour unit the survivor is the one carrying more overrides — the edited one holds information. And re-running it when nothing has moved is a no-op, or the routine that repairs a scene is the routine that corrupts it.

On the reading side, `inspect_formwork_parts` now carries `coversWholeElement` and `coverageCaveat`, and the system prompt says to lead with the caveat rather than the bill. This is the one field that invalidates every figure above it: a takeoff for four of an element's twelve pours is not a small error, and each of its numbers is individually correct.

**One pre-existing bug the tests found on the way.** `duplicateMarks` was checked flat across an element's shutters, but a mark encodes station and elevation *within its own pour unit* — so the same panel in two lifts of one wall carries the same mark by design. A correctly shuttered three-lift wall reported every panel it had as a clash. Now checked per shutter, the way the parts panel always did it, and each entry names its assembly. A clash list that fills up whenever an element is split is a clash list nobody reads.

Covered by 9 reconciliation tests in `formwork-assembly/attach.test.ts` (3 mutation-verified) and 15 in `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai-formwork-reattach.test.ts` (4 mutation-verified — appending instead of reconciling fails 9 of them). The `chat-ai-formwork-settings.test.ts` assertion that the reply asks for a re-attach was inverted, since it was pinning the destructive behaviour.

## What landed: the tie that was never drawn

The first of phase 9's clash passes, and the whole of the work was working out that **the clash is not the finding**. `passable()` in `geometry-wall.ts` already refuses to place a tie whose station lands inside an opening, and it is right to: a rod through a void bears on nothing. So an invariant asserting "a tie hits a window" would report a condition the model never produces — it would fire on every wall with a door, and the fix it named would already be applied.

The reportable defect is what refusing leaves behind. A band of concrete beside an opening — a pier between a jamb and a wall end, a head band over a door — can contain **no drilled hole at all**, while being loaded on both faces at full pressure. On a panel system there is nothing to add there, because the frames arrive drilled: the answer is a strut through the box-out or a strongback spanning the opening. That makes it a **warning** rather than an error, since `severity` here means buildability and this is buildable by a detail somebody signs.

**The evidence had to be recorded before it was filtered**, which is the inverse of every other piece of `ShutterEvidence`. A tie never drawn leaves no part, so the emitted ties can never answer the question — `TieField.holes` is the two skins' drilled grid intersected and *unfiltered* by openings, because the openings are the validator's own input and removing them here would hide exactly the loss the check asserts about. Filtering it would be a check that always passes, which this file already records four instances of.

**Three bounds on a tie field, each of which would otherwise report a fault that isn't there.** It is one field per *panelled stretch*, not one per element: a pour unit's stations say nothing about the stretch the next unit forms, and merging them calls a band tied by a hole in a different pour. A corner unit interrupts the panel run and ties through its own holes on the catalog's spacing, so the stretch under it is excluded rather than reported as untied. And both faces' corner runs are used, so every stretch is one the panels form on *both* skins — a rod needs a hole at each end.

**Four judgements in the check itself**, all of them about not producing noise:

- **Rows need a tolerance** (`ROW_TOLERANCE_M = 0.02`), not exact equality. Two runs on one face can be packed with different panel widths and a narrow panel is drilled at its own levels, so the same physical row arrives as two elevations a millimetre apart. Split, each half carries only some of the row's stations and a band tied by the other half reads as untied.
- **`MIN_TIED_BAND_MM = 300` is a site fact, not a tolerance.** A nib that narrow is strutted form to form, which is what a carpenter does with a 200 mm pier; reporting it would put a finding on every wall whose opening lands near its end.
- **One finding per band, not per row.** A full-height opening fails its pier at every course, and four findings about one 800 mm band reads as four problems. The rows are counted in the message and the *lowest* is the locus, because pressure is worst at the base.
- **Only the rows the opening crosses are cut.** A door head at 2.1 m takes nothing out of the row at 2.7 m, and treating every opening as a full-height cut is how a wall with a low window gets reported as untied at the top.

A band bounded only by the shutter's own ends is left to `tieGrid`'s `untied-stretch` — a different fault with a different fix, and reported here too it would be one problem in two lists. Conventional shutters are exempt (`holes.length === 0`): the carpenter bores the ply where the calculation asks, so there is no fixed grid to fall between.

**The check is a function of the layout, not of the opening**, which is the property that makes it worth having and is pinned in all three suites. The same 6 m wall with the same 4.8 m window is faulted in 2.70 m panels — drilled at 1.35 and 4.65 m, so every station is in the void and both piers are untied — and silent in 0.6 m panels, which bring a hole every 300 mm. A check keyed on the opening alone would fault both, and a user who narrowed the panels to fix it would watch the finding stay.

Covered by 12 tests in `validate/invariants.test.ts` keyed on the catalog's real hole positions rather than invented ones (so a catalog change is a test failure, not a check that quietly stops describing the wall), 4 in `validate-project.test.ts` including the field asserted to *keep* the stations an opening blocks, and 2 in `chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts`. Neither surface changed: `FindingRow` already renders a multi-id finding as "2 elements" for `OPENING_STRADDLES_LIFT_JOINT`, and `notChecked` renders generically off `INVARIANT_LABELS`.

## What landed: the questions, askable from outside the editor

The claim in the last section of this file was that the port was thin over shared modules and the work was the tool surface. That held — no engine function changed — but the shape of it was not where the difficulty was. `packages/mcp` runs under Bun with no DOM, and the two functions it needed were behind `formwork-assembly/index.ts`, which reaches React, `@pascal-app/viewer`, `@pascal-app/editor`, `lucide-react` and `zustand`, because the panels live there too.

**The solve itself is headless already, and that was worth establishing empirically rather than assuming either way.** The geometry builders construct `Group`/`Mesh`/`BoxGeometry`, and the part collector reads marks off those meshes — which is the whole reason the bill and the on-screen shutter cannot count differently. `three` builds all of it without a browser and the meshes are then discarded. Measured through the barrel the import cost ~1026 ms; through the pure modules, ~81 ms. So the answer was not `export_glb`'s answer (decline in headless mode), because the solve demonstrably runs and produces the editor's own numbers.

`formwork-assembly/headless.ts` and the `@pascal-app/nodes/formwork-assembly/headless` subpath export are that narrow door: `solveProjectFormwork`, `validateProjectFormwork` and their types, reaching `@pascal-app/core/{formwork,registry,schema}` and `three` and nothing else. **It is a re-export, not a second implementation** — a headless *copy* of the solve is the same divergence hazard `parts.ts` and `solve.ts` were each written against, one layer further out.

**The narrowness is enforced, not documented.** `headless.test.ts` walks the import graph from the entry point over the *source* — so it fails in the editor the moment the import is typed, rather than after a build on someone else's machine — and fails if the chain reaches any package outside that permitted set. A companion test asserts the barrel *does* reach React, so a reviewer reading only `headless.ts` cannot conclude the distinction is decorative. The failure this guards is silent by construction: add `import { FormworkBom } from './parts-summary'` to `solve.ts` and every existing test still passes while the MCP install grows a UI stack.

**The entry point was right and under-used, which cost a broken production build.** This section framed the barrel's weight as an MCP cold-start problem. It is also a hard error one layer over: a Next Route Handler *is* a React Server Component, so a client hook anywhere in its import graph fails the compile rather than merely costing milliseconds. `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai.ts` imported `castableHostIds` and six siblings from the barrel, and `buildSolverJointNodes` from `construction-joint/index.ts`, whose node definition carries the parametric editors — so `editor#build` failed with 55 copies of one error, an import trace running the chat route through the validation panel into every tool in `@pascal-app/editor`. Nothing else showed it. `bun run check-types` and `bun test` both import React without complaint, so the tree carried a build that could not ship while every gate read green.

Fixed by widening the door rather than by adding a second one: `pourUnitsForHost`, `reconcileFormworkNodes` and `solveShuttersForHost` were already headless — `attach.ts` and `solve.ts` reach `@pascal-app/core/*` and `three` and nothing else — so they are now re-exported from `formwork-assembly/headless.ts`, and `construction-joint/headless.ts` is the same narrow door onto `buildSolverJointNodes`. `apps/editor/lib/server-imports.test.ts` walks every `app/api/**/route.ts` and fails on a client-only barrel, naming the entry point to use instead; a companion assertion checks the chat route still reaches the solve, so the guard cannot be satisfied by a route that has stopped doing the work. `bun run build` is now recorded in `AGENTS.md` as a fourth gate alongside types, tests and lint, because it is the only one that sees this class of fault.

**The same build was also shipping 81 MB of the wrong thing, and said so in six warnings nobody read.** With the errors cleared, `editor#build` still reported "Encountered unexpected file in NFT list … the whole project was traced unintentionally" against every route that imports `@pascal-app/mcp`'s scene store. That is Next's file tracer, which decides what a serverless bundle needs to carry: `SqliteSceneStore`'s constructor calls `path.resolve` on a database path chosen at runtime from the environment, and a `resolve` over a value the tracer cannot see through reads as *this module may open anything*. So it took the whole project. The chat route's trace was 912 files and 85.7 MB, 81.2 MB of it `public/` glTF and KTX2 — model assets, in a JSON API handler. `/api/health`, which imports no store, traced 149 files and nothing from `public/`, which is what identified the cause.

One `/*turbopackIgnore: true*/` inside that `resolve` call, verified by patching the emitted `dist` and rebuilding before touching the source: 912 files → 158, 85.7 MB → 3.0 MB, six warnings → none, and the other two routes the same. Nothing is lost, because the path genuinely points outside the project. The comment is the whole fix, which makes it the one kind of comment `tsc` could drop with every test still green, so `sqlite-scene-store.test.ts` asserts it survives into `dist/` — a formatter or a `removeComments` flag would otherwise restore the 81 MB silently, and a fat deploy is not a failing build.

**Two deliberate divergences from the chat tools, both MCP idiom rather than new behaviour.** A bad level id is refused with `isError: true` and no `structuredContent` — legal even with an `outputSchema` declared, because the SDK's `validateToolOutput` returns early on an error result — and the refusal says `Call list_levels and read the id of the level you mean.`, since MCP has no `list_castable_elements` to point at. Everything else is shared verbatim: `validationSummary` and `projectFormworkCaveats` come from core, and the suite asserts the tool's `summary` is `===` the panel's, so the server, the panels and the chat cannot drift into a third phrasing of one fault.

**The agent guide is where the system prompt would have gone.** MCP hosts give a server no system prompt, so the reasoning the chat surface states in `SYSTEM_PROMPT` — that formwork is a different model from the building, that neither answer is derivable from `get_scene`, that the two severity counts must never be merged, that `notChecked` and `caveats` are always reported — lives in `pascal://agent-guide` and in the tool descriptions instead. Without it a model holding a scene graph will try to infer a takeoff from wall areas, which is the one failure mode this whole surface exists to prevent.

Verified end to end against the compiled stdio binary rather than only the registrar: 9 BOM lines and 2433.8 kg on a probe wall, the `OPENING_LEAVES_TIE_GAP` finding with its strut advice, five `notChecked` entries, and `levelId: 'level_9'` refused. The probe also caught the one thing a unit test would not have: `create_wall` does not set `formworkType`, so an unformed wall bills nothing — which is exactly the "reads as a floor that needs nothing" case `unshuttered` was added for, arriving unprompted through the tool's own front door.

## What landed: an AI edit the user can take back

The row said "chat mutations bypass undo" and prescribed `runAsSingleSceneHistoryStep`. Both halves were off, and the way they were off is the reason this is recorded rather than just fixed.

**The defect was destruction, not omission.** An agent's result reaches the client over SSE and was applied with `applySceneGraphToEditor`, which is the *load* path and correctly ends in `clearSceneHistory()` — its own comment explains why, and the reason is sound: loading is itself a tracked write, so without the reset a few Ctrl+Z presses step past the load into the previous project, or into an empty scene, and take the whole thing with them. Used for an AI edit, that same reset threw away the history the edit should have been appended to. So the user pressed Ctrl+Z after an AI edit, nothing happened, and their own work from before it was already gone. **There is no error state in that** — an empty history is indistinguishable from a history with nothing left to undo, which is why a defect that loses work outright sat behind a row reading like a missing nicety.

**The prescription pointed at a layer that cannot run it.** `apps/editor/lib/chat-ai.ts` mutates a plain JSON graph, not the store, and the route explains why: core's zustand store is `'use client'`, which Next's Route Handler bundler turns into a client-reference stub the moment it is actually touched server-side. There is no store on the server to wrap a transaction around. The fix has to be where a store exists — the client listener — and the fix is a *second* apply path rather than a change to the first, because clearing history is right for a load and wrong for an edit, and one function cannot be both.

`applyAgentSceneGraphToEditor` is that path, and it takes **every** event the live listener accepts. The first draft gated it on the event's `kind` against an allowlist of `ai_chat` and `ai_construction_plan`, which was wrong twice over. It missed ~21 kinds: MCP's mutation tools each publish under their own tool name — `create_wall`, `apply_patch`, `delete_node`, `furnish_room` and the rest through `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` — and every one of those is equally an agent editing the scene the user is watching. And it was unnecessary, because the listener already discards anything that is not *this* scene at a *higher* version, so nothing arriving on that channel is a load. The three genuine loads — mount, project switch, version preview — are all inside `<Editor>` and still clear history. An allowlist would have quietly withheld undo from the next tool anyone added, which is the same class of silent failure as the original bug.

**Collapsing the step, not merely tracking it, and the distinction is testable.** A populated graph is a single tracked `set` inside `setScene`, so tracking alone looks correct on every ordinary edit — and zundo's `equality: areSceneSnapshotsEqual` already drops the no-op case, so a read-only turn costs nothing either way. The collapse earns its keep on exactly one path: an **empty** graph takes `clearScene()` + `setInstalledPlugins()`, two tracked writes, and two entries mean the first Ctrl+Z appears to do nothing, so the user presses again and loses an edit they wanted. That is the one assertion that distinguishes the shipped behaviour from the plausible-but-wrong version of it.

Covered by `packages/editor/src/lib/agent-scene-history.test.ts` (6), and the suite was verified against two mutations rather than one, because the two failures are different. Reverting to the load path fails **all six**. Tracking without collapsing fails exactly **one** — the empty-graph test — which is the point of having it: without that case the suite would pass on an implementation that costs two presses for one action. Two of the six also needed strengthening for the same reason the original defect was invisible: asserting `pastStates` is empty after a read-only turn passes just as well when history has been wiped, so that test now puts an entry on the stack first and asserts it *survives*.

Nothing about the formwork engine changed, and no formwork test moved. It is in this file because every formwork mutation the AI can make — `attach_formwork`, `set_formwork_part`, `set_pour_limits`, both settings writers — travelled this path, and a tool that reshuffles a wall's shutters is a poor thing to be unable to undo. It also removed a reason not to ship the MCP formwork mutations: the "live-snapshot publish" they were deferred behind lands as an undoable step for free, since `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` goes down the same listener. The first of them, `set_formwork_settings`, has since shipped on exactly that basis.

The doc row that prescribed the fix is worth keeping in mind as a pattern: it named a real function and a real problem, and following it literally would have produced a server-side change that cannot compile against a client store, for a defect one order of magnitude worse than the row described. The row was written from the symptom.

## What landed: two corner units where there is room for one

Phase 9's second clash pass, and the mirror image of the first. Ties × openings had to be reported as the *loss* rather than the collision, because the layout already refuses to draw a rod into a void. Here the layout does not refuse anything, and that is the whole defect.

**The takeoff was internally consistent and wrong.** A wall between two returns carries a corner unit at each end, and each leg eats inward from its own junction. `panelRuns` (`geometry-wall.ts`) subtracts each `corner.blocked` stretch in turn and drops runs under `PANEL_GAP`, so two legs claiming the same concrete produce a *shorter panel run*, not an open one. No `unfilledMm`, therefore no `UNFORMABLE_STRIP`; a bill listing two corner units for a return with room for one; and every figure agreeing with every other. Nothing in the product contradicted anything, which is exactly why this needed an invariant and not a fix — there was no inconsistency for a stricter check elsewhere to catch. On a 200 mm wall in Framax the inside legs are clear at 1.0 m and overlap from about 0.79 m down; the outside legs, longer by the core, are already unformable at 0.7 m.

**Two findings, because the fault has two shapes.** `CORNER_UNITS_OVERLAP` is the geometry: a warning normally, since notching one unit back or forming the return as a single bespoke box is a decision rather than a layout, and an **error** where the legs need more of the face than the face has, because then there is no stretch between them to notch back to. `OPENING_INSIDE_CORNER_UNIT` is the plan's "opening too near a corner → custom": a jamb *inside* a leg's extent, where there is no panel joint to move and the box-out has to be cut into a framed unit. A warning, because which answer applies turns on whether the plant is owned or hired and no field records that.

**Reported per face, and the two faces genuinely disagree.** An outside leg wraps the core, so it is longer than its inside counterpart by the wall thickness — 500 mm against 300 on a 200 mm wall. A 700 mm link is therefore two different problems at once: face `a` needs 600 mm of a 700 mm face and its units can be notched, face `b` needs 1000 and cannot. One finding averaged over both would name a stretch that is on neither skin, which is why a single wall can legitimately produce one error and one warning.

**The leg placement moved into core rather than being derived twice.** `cornerLegExtent` in `coverage/junctions.ts` is now the one answer to where a leg lands, read by both `geometry-shared.ts`'s `cornerRuns` (which places the unit) and the validator's `cornerLegsByFace` (which asks whether two collide). The caller still resolves the leg *length*, because only it knows the system — the catalog's fitted leg where a unit sweeps the angle, the derived length where none does and the corner is cut on site. Computed separately, a check and the thing it checks drift, which is the hazard `parts.ts` was written against one layer down. The placement has two subtleties that are wrong in opposite directions if either is dropped: the leg runs toward the element's end or back toward its start, and it starts at the corner of the *concrete* rather than the centreline — which is what makes an outside leg's first joint land at the same station as its inside counterpart's, so a tie between the skins passes through square.

**Three judgements, each pinned by a test that fails without it.** Legs that meet *exactly* are the intended geometry on a return twice the leg length, so the check needs a tolerance — `LEG_OVERLAP_TOLERANCE_M = 0.002`, matching the width at which the layout already calls two panel edges one joint; without it a wall dimensioned 799 mm instead of 800 is a finding. Unformed corners are skipped, because `formed` is monolithic-only and a sequenced junction gets a bulkhead rather than a unit, so checking the legs a monolithic pour *would* have had reports hardware nobody sets. And the opening check tests strict containment rather than a comfort distance: an opening 50 mm off a corner unit is the ordinary case and forms with a filler, so a proximity rule would fault walls the crew builds daily.

**Neither pair needs a shuttered layout, unlike the tie gap, and so neither ever appears in `notChecked`.** Both shipped systems turn a right angle on the same 300 mm leg — PERI's TE 270-2 is 180 × 300, Framax's corners are 300 — so `DEFAULT_INSIDE_CORNER_LEG_M` is the figure they agree on rather than a guess, and a scene that has never opened the formwork settings is told its 500 mm return is unformable just the same. Listing these as unchecked would send the reader off to form something before an answer that is already available.

Covered by 14 tests in `validate/invariants.test.ts`, keyed on the catalog's real leg lengths so a catalog change is a test failure rather than a check that quietly stops describing the wall; 2 in `validate-project.test.ts` (through the solve, and asserting the pair is absent from `notChecked`); 1 in `formwork.test.ts`; and 2 in `chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts`. Six mutations were tried and each killed a different test: dropping the unformable escalation, removing the overlap tolerance, swapping strict containment for a 300 mm comfort distance, naming only the wall instead of its neighbours, ignoring the face split, and skipping the `formed` check. Neither validation surface changed — `FindingRow` already renders a multi-id finding and `notChecked` renders generically off `INVARIANT_LABELS`.

## What landed: a tie rod through the water seal

**`TIE_THROUGH_WATERSTOP` — the third clash pass, and the one where every other check in the product reports a pass.** A drilled tie hole inside the width of a waterstop is a rod through the seal. The tie is within its capacity, the grid is symmetric, the run closes, the pressure design holds, the bill totals: there is no figure anywhere in the solve that disagrees with any other. The wall is simply not watertight, and nobody finds out until the tank is filled.

**The reason it is reachable rather than dormant, which is the discovery that shaped the check.** `hardCutsForElement` in `pours/units.ts` cuts a pour only at `expansion` and `isolation` joints, because a construction joint is a *soft* partition the solver may move. So a construction joint carrying a waterstop is **not** a shutter boundary: the panel run crosses it and takes the drilled tie grid across with it. Nothing between the bar and the frame's factory holes negotiates, and neither of them knows the other exists. Four of this suite's first drafts could not fail against any scene, so establishing that this one *can* was the first piece of work rather than the last.

**An error, not a warning, and that is the one severity judgement in the suite with no site answer.** Every other clash has something a foreman chooses on the day — strut the box-out, notch the corner unit, accept a bespoke corner. Here the fix is upstream of the shutter: the bar moves, the tie row moves, or the tie becomes a watertight assembly (a taper tie, or a sealed DK cone plugged and patched — `TieType.watertight` in the catalog, three to five times the cost per tie point). Proceeding is not an exception somebody signs; it is a leak nobody sees.

**Six further judgements, each pinned by a test that a deliberate mutation killed.** An *interface* joint is skipped, because the bar at a joint between two elements sits in the plane where they meet, the panels stop at that plane, and there is no drilled hole over it to compare. An `injectable-hose` treatment is excluded, because a grouted tube is sealed after the pour by injection rather than by an unbroken bar. The width comes from the type when nobody states one — PVC bar is sold in 150/200/250 mm and a hydrophilic strip is a ~25 mm section, an order of magnitude between two treatments the same field would otherwise describe identically — and a stated `JointTreatment.width` overrides it, which is why the field was added to the schema in this change and to the node's own `.describe()` block so the AI can set it. The comparison runs on the axis the bar actually occupies: `along` for a vertical pour break, `elevation` for a horizontal lift joint, so a horizontal bar is compared against tie *rows* and not tie columns. And it is deliberately **not** gated on `exposureClass === 'water-retaining'`, unlike `WATERSTOP_RUN_NOT_CLOSED`: that check asks about a water-retaining *envelope*, while this one asks about a bar somebody has actually specified, and a basement wall leaks through a tie hole whatever its exposure class says.

**The locus is on the bar, not on one of the holes crossing it.** One finding per bar rather than one per hole, counting the crossings in the message, because the fix is to the joint or to the tie assembly and both are about the bar — a locus on one crossing of three sends the reader to a hole that is not the problem. The test for this needed the fixture bar offset from the hole (2.95 m against a hole at 3.00), or both loci are the same number and the assertion cannot tell them apart; that is how the mutation survived the first draft of the test.

Covered by 16 tests in `validate/invariants.test.ts`, keyed on the catalog's real drilled stations — Framax's `TIE_SPACING_MM = 1350` puts a 6 m face's holes at 1.35 / 3.00 / 4.65 m along and 0.775 / 2.125 m up, so a catalog change is a test failure rather than a check that quietly stops describing the wall; 2 in `validate-project.test.ts` (through the real solve, and the same bar between two tie columns staying silent); and 1 in `chat-ai-formwork-validate.test.ts` asserting the model is told both ways out. Seven mutations were tried and each killed a different test. Neither validation surface changed: `FindingRow` already renders a multi-id finding and `notChecked` renders generically off `INVARIANT_LABELS`.

## What landed: whose rack the bill comes off

**The owned/hired split — `systems/formwork/supply.ts`.** A bill that says a job needs 260 panels does not say what it costs, and the first fact a costing pass needs is not a price: it is which of the 260 the yard already has. `bomSupply(lines, owned)` answers it per line — `ownedQuantity`, `hiredQuantity`, `consumedQuantity`, and `hiredModifiedQuantity` beside them.

**Ownership is a pool, not a flag, and that is why this is engine work rather than a label.** A yard owning 200 of a panel type and needing 260 hires 60. A boolean per type would put all 260 on hire the moment the job outgrew the rack by one, so the answer for a line is a *split*, and it depends on what earlier lines of the same catalog id already took. The allocation order is itself a cost decision: `CONDITION_PRIORITY` spends owned stock on `modified` lines first, because a hired panel returned with holes in it is recharged at list while the yard's own panel is already the yard's problem. Allocated the other way round, the same job hands five drilled panels back to a hire company.

**Absent stock is not zero stock, and this is the one resolved `FormworkSettings` field deliberately left undefaulted.** `{ owned: {} }` is a real answer — "we own nothing, price it all as hire". *Absent* means nobody has said, and then the takeoff shows no split at all rather than a bill reading "everything on hire", which is a claim about the yard that the project never made. It is the same discipline as the validator's `notChecked`, `bomWeightKg`'s `complete`, and the design report's "assumed" versus "project". It also makes `mergeFormworkOwnedStock` necessary: the generic group merge replaces `owned` wholesale, so recording one panel type would forget the rest of the yard, and unlike `mergeFormworkCement` it *keeps* an emptied rack as `stock: {}`. The panel's "Back to nobody having said" button is therefore a separate control rather than a side effect of removing the last line.

**Two scopes are not additive, and both the caveats and every tool description say so.** Level 1 and level 2 each drawing the same 100 owned panels is correct if they are cast in sequence. A per-level answer is right per level and two of them must not be added — the same hazard `bomLines` already carries about the bill itself, arriving through the same door rather than as a new one. Reuse across sequential pours was phase 10's set counting and has since landed, and the caveat stands **unchanged** rather than being softened by it: the count is over the pours *in this scope*, so two scopes' peaks are no more additive than their owned figures were, and it is the reason the count is reported beside the split rather than folded into it.

**It reaches every surface that shows a bill, in the same words.** `solveProjectFormwork` carries `supply?: BomSupply`, computed only where a rack is stated; `projectFormworkCaveats` gained the per-scope warning and the recharge warning, so the panel, the CSV and both AI surfaces phrase them identically. `bom-csv.ts` splices three columns after Quantity and three preamble rows, and omits them entirely with no rack — a column of blanks under "Hire" is the most confident wrong answer that file could give. `takeoff-panel.tsx` shows a Supply section, per-line splits only where a line is actually split, and a note where nothing has been recorded. `settings-fields.tsx`'s `StockRackField` is a list somebody adds to rather than a field per catalog id: there are hundreds of ownable parts and a yard owns a handful, so a field per id would be a wall of blanks in which "assumed" is the answer everywhere.

**`STOCKABLE_CATALOG_PARTS` exists so the panel and the AI cannot disagree about what is ownable.** Two hand-built id lists would agree on the day they were written and drift the first time a system joined the catalog, and that failure shows up as a project recording stock against an id no bill line can match — accepted, stored, changing no quantity at all. Composing it turned up a latent barrel cycle: `stockable.ts` reading `FORMWORK_SYSTEMS` from `catalog/index.ts`, which re-exports `stockable`, would build the id list before the systems record was assigned and silently omit every system's panels. `catalog/systems.ts` was extracted for that reason and records it.

**A bespoke part can carry a `catalogId`, which a surviving mutant taught.** `geometry-slab.ts:202` emits a cut deck sheet with the sheet-stock id on it — the id names the stock it was cut *from*, not a rackable part. The first fixture set both `provenance: 'bespoke'` and `catalogId: undefined`, so the catalog-less clause masked the bespoke clause and a mutation to either survived; the test is now split so each clause has its own killer. The second survivor was a `fromStock > 0` guard around a `drawnOn.add` that nothing could observe, and it was deleted rather than tested.

Covered by 19 tests in `supply.test.ts` (every one of 12 + 2 mutants killed), 10 in core's new `settings.test.ts` over `mergeFormworkOwnedStock` and the resolver, 7 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 6 in `solve-project.test.ts` plus 3 more caveat tests, 2 in `takeoff.test.ts`, 5 in `use-formwork-settings.test.ts`, 9 in `chat-ai-formwork-settings.test.ts`, 4 in `chat-ai-project-formwork.test.ts` and 2 in the MCP suite. `inspect_formwork_settings` also reads the rack back, which it could not before: a field the AI can write and not read is one it cannot check its own work against.

## What landed: how long the bill is held

**The striking time — `design/striking.ts` and `hire.ts`.** The owned/hired split answered *which* of the 260 panels are hired. This answers *for how long*, and the two together are a rental charge with only the rate missing. It stops there deliberately: a period times an invented rate is a price, and a price nobody can trace is the thing this whole feature is written against.

**This is the one figure in the design chain that is not a property of the form**, and the consequence is a second temperature field. Every other module here asks what the concrete does to the shutter; this asks what the shutter waits for the concrete to do. So the sensitivity runs the *opposite* way — a colder mix raises the pressure the form is designed for, and a colder cure lengthens the time it is held. `Placement.concreteTemperatureC` is the mix as it arrives and `Curing.surfaceTemperatureC` is the surface while it cures, and concrete placed at 20 °C into a form standing in 4 °C air does not cure at 20. **One field would have been wrong for one of the two answers whichever value it held**, which is why the panel's own note says so and both the schema's and the tool's descriptions name the other field by name.

**Three criteria exist and only the tabulated one is implemented, which the module says in its own header.** ACI §3.7 is explicit that strength is the preferred basis and elapsed time the fallback, so it is worth being plain: *strength* (~70 % `f'c`) needs a design strength no node carries and is contract-specific rather than code-mandated; *maturity* (Nurse–Saul, equivalent age) needs a temperature history and an `M_target` calibrated from job-cured specimens, and a maturity function with a guessed target is a check that cannot fail — this file already documents four of those. *Elapsed time* is what the codes tabulate, and it is what this is.

**The period is per thing-struck, not per element, and that distinction is the module's reason to exist.** BS 8110 gives a slab's soffit form 100/(t+10) days and the props beneath it 250; ACI says the same thing in footnote ‡, where a form that leaves its shores behind comes off at half the time — the clause the entire drophead market exists on. A per-element period reports the deck as held for the props' period, two and a half times too long, and prices a hire accordingly. So `StrikeTarget` has five rows, including the two beam ones that are unreachable from a scene today: a transcription that silently keeps two rows of four is worse than one that keeps all four, because the missing ones get filled in later from memory.

**`basis` exists because the two families do not measure the same clock.** ACI's periods are cumulative time above 10 °C, *not necessarily consecutive* — so 4 ACI days is a fortnight in a cold spring — and BS's are calendar days at a stated surface temperature. Reporting either as "days" is how a programme promises a strike date the concrete has not agreed to, which is why it is carried as a warning as well as a field: a reader who does not know cannot ask.

**Two adjustments both codes require and neither quantifies, reported rather than applied.** High-early-strength concrete permits a reduction "as approved" and a retarder or a cold spell requires an increase "at the discretion of the engineer", both with no factor attached. Inventing one would put a fabricated number into the figure that decides when a floor is allowed to carry itself. The reduction is reported as *available* rather than taken, which is the direction that costs hire rather than the direction that drops a slab. `delayedSetting` is not a field anybody enters, either: a retarder is already recorded on the mix because it is worth 20 % of the pressure, so `strikingInputFor` reads the mix and adds the codes' other trigger, a cure below 10 °C. Asked separately, a project could state a retarder for its pressure and not for its strike time.

**DIN publishes no striking table at all, and the substitution is stated rather than performed quietly.** Its family answers removal in EN 13670 §5.5, which is open item 4 and genuinely uncovered, so a DIN project falls to BS 8110's formulas. `strikingStandardFor` is therefore a *mapping* from the pressure standard rather than the same field — a pressure standard is picked because a panel's rating is certified against it, a striking table because a contract cites it. DIN is the shipped default, so `strikingStandardSubstituted` and its caveat fire on a project nobody has touched.

**The join to the bill is the marks, and nothing re-derives anything.** A period is per `StrikeTarget`; a bill line is per catalog id at one condition. `bomHire` takes `targetsForMark` from the only layer that knows a part's host — `solve-project.ts`, because core's `strikeTargetForPartKind` can say a prop under a slab is a shore and a prop against a wall is a raker, and cannot tell them apart on its own. This is the same discipline as `validate-project.ts` taking its evidence out of the build pass: a second enumeration is how the screen and the AI come to disagree. It also solves one period per *target* rather than per line, since re-solving the same formula for each of a 200-line bill is the same arithmetic 200 times.

**A line can span two periods, and that is reported rather than averaged.** This is the case the module is built around. A prop is a prop: the same catalog id props a slab soffit and rakes a wall, and `bomLines` groups them because to a delivery note they are one line — but the shores are held ten days and the rakers twelve hours. All three plausible single answers are wrong in a way the number cannot show: the mean under-charges the shores, the max over-charges the rakers, and first-mark-wins depends on solve order. So `mixed` is reported and `hours` is the longest, because over-stating a hire costs money and under-stating it strikes a floor early. `targetsForMark` returns a *list* for the same reason a mark is not unique across a project — `duplicateMarks` exists for that, and a colliding mark makes its line honestly mixed instead of silently keeping whichever host sorted last.

**`longestHours` is never a sum**, and every surface says so in its own words. A set's occupancy is set by its slowest release: the props holding one slab do not shorten the props holding the next, and adding periods together produces a duration longer than the job. The CSV's total row reads `"longest, not a total"` in the days column for exactly this reason, because a column of days is a column a spreadsheet sums.

**Absent is not zero, again.** A tie is cut off inside the wall and a drum of release agent is gone, so those lines carry no period at all: `hours` undefined, CSV `not struck`, `daysHeld: null` on both AI surfaces. A `0` prices spent material as plant returned the same day, and it would enter a `longestHours` comparison as though it were an answer. `complete` is false where any line has no period — which is a statement about the *bill*, not about the tie.

**`curing` is the one resolved settings group deliberately left undefaulted**, against the rule every pressure input follows. The striking tables default *themselves*: BS prints a "16 °C and above" column, ACI a longest span band, and `strikingTime` takes the conservative end and names it in `assumed`. A number resolved in `FormworkSettings` would reach the same figure and lose the only thing distinguishing it from one the job stated. Same discipline as the validator's `notChecked`, `bomWeightKg`'s `complete` and the design report's "assumed" versus "project".

**It reaches every surface a bill reaches, in the same words.** `ProjectFormwork.hire` is always present; `projectFormworkCaveats` carries the striking warnings verbatim and the mixed-line messages per line, so a figure and the reason it may be wrong travel together. `bom-csv.ts` splices two columns after the supply split and four preamble row types (the standard, one `Period —` row per distinct period with its clause, the `ASSUMED` rows, and the mixed marker on the line), and omits all of it where the caller solved no periods — an element-scope export has no project settings to read, and two blank columns headed "Days held" read as plant nobody keeps. The takeoff panel gets a "Held for" section in the unit a crew reads (12 h, not 0.5 d). Both AI surfaces carry `daysHeld`/`struckAs` per line and a `hire` block, with three prohibitions in the tool description because a column of days invites exactly three wrong operations. And the settings panel's new "Curing" group closes the parity: without it the model could state a January cure and the user could not.

**`bomHire` is on the `./formwork` subpath only, and that is the correct answer rather than an omission.** It went onto the root barrel first, which turned out to be uncallable there: its signature takes `StrikingStandardId` and `StrikeTarget`, and the root barrel deliberately carries no part of the design chain's vocabulary. An exported function whose argument types are not reachable is worse than an absent one, because it type-checks at the export and fails at the call. No consumer imports any `bom*` function from the root barrel — every one of them uses `@pascal-app/core/formwork` — so the entry was withdrawn.

Covered by 27 tests in `design/striking.test.ts`, 12 in `hire.test.ts`, 9 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 3 in core's `settings.test.ts`, 10 in `solve-project.test.ts`, 3 in `takeoff.test.ts`, 1 in `use-formwork-settings.test.ts`, 3 in `chat-ai-formwork-settings.test.ts`, 5 in `chat-ai-project-formwork.test.ts` and 3 in the MCP suite.

**Two test premises were wrong in a way worth recording, because both are about the engine rather than about syntax.** An "omitted part carries no period" test asserted something the engine does not and should not do: `bomLines` drops an omitted part from the quantity *and* from the marks, so omitting one of four braces leaves the line intact. The honest invariant is that the bill pass and the `targetsByMark` pass share one omission filter, so a fully-omitted line disappears from both — which is what the test now asserts. And an "ACI assumed something" test conflated two code paths: ACI's vertical row is a flat 12 h with no lookup, so its `clear-span` and `load-ratio` assumptions fire only when it consults the *soffit* table, and a wall-only scene legitimately assumes nothing. The assumption worth asserting there is BS 8110's default temperature column, on the shipped default.

## What landed: what the bill costs to hold

**The hire cost — `systems/formwork/cost.ts`, and a rate table the project records.** The split answered *which* of the 260 panels are hired and the striking time answered *for how long*. This is the third factor, and it is the first item in the whole feature whose missing input was commercial rather than structural: the two before it stopped short on purpose, because a period times an invented rate is a price and a price nobody can trace is the thing this feature is written against. So this module adds no rate of its own. Every figure it produces is a rate somebody typed times a quantity the engine already derived.

**The rate lives on the project, and that was the decision the whole change hung off.** A `CatalogEntry` carries `weightKg`, `catalogSource` and `verification` and deliberately carries no price. The same Framax panel is different money to two yards in the same city, different money next quarter, and different again under a framework agreement — so a rate shipped in seed data would drift with the catalog edition while looking exactly as authoritative as the weights beside it. `FormworkRateSettings` is therefore a settings group like the rack: `byCatalogId`, plus the two terms that belong to the *agreement* rather than to any product — the currency and the minimum hire period. Repeating a minimum hire period against forty ids is thirty-nine copies that go stale.

**Three costs, because they are three different events, and summing them alone would hide all three.** A hire charge is time-based and reversible — the panel goes back. A recharge is a purchase nobody decided to make: a hired panel this pour drilled does not return as stock, so the hire company invoices it at list. A consumable is spent. The first is negotiable on programme, the second is avoidable by allocating the yard's own stock to the drilled work (which `CONDITION_PRIORITY` already does), and the third is fixed by the design. They are reported apart *and* totalled, because a total is what a tender needs and the three apart are what a decision needs.

**Absent is the answer, and here it is sharper than anywhere else in this file.** `curing` is undefaulted because the striking tables print their own conservative column; the rack is undefaulted because "we own nothing" is a real answer somebody has to give. A rate is undefaulted for a harder reason: **there is no conservative fallback at all.** Guess low and the job under-bids; guess high and it loses the tender. So a project that has recorded nothing gets no money on its takeoff whatsoever — no `cost` block, no `lineCost`, no zero. A `0` is the single number a model or a reader would repeat as a price, and the one figure in this model that cannot be withdrawn once it has been sent.

**A stated table with nothing in it is the other answer**, following the rack exactly: `rates: { byCatalogId: {} }` is a project that opened the table and priced nothing, where absent is nobody having been asked. That distinction is what makes `mergeFormworkRates` a *field-level* merge rather than the rack's row-level one — a list price comes off a price list and a hire term off an agreement, entered at different times by different people, and replacing the rate object would make filling in the second delete the first. `null` against a field clears just that field; an emptied rate drops the id, because no number means "priced, amount unknown"; and the group survives an emptied table.

**The minimum hire period is most of the answer on a fast cycle.** A wall form struck in 12 hours against a 28-day minimum is charged for 28 days — not a rounding but a 56× difference on that line, and the single most common reason a hire invoice does not reconcile with a programme. `daysCharged` rather than `daysHeld` is therefore what every surface reports as the billable figure, `linesAtMinimum` names the lines it caught, and the caveat says outright that the remedy is a planning decision: pour more with the same set, or negotiate the term. Striking sooner saves nothing on those lines.

**Owned stock is excluded rather than priced at zero, and the exclusion is named.** Pricing a panel the yard already holds needs a reuse count per *panel type*, which nothing in the catalog carries — `SheetStock.expectedReuses` exists for sheets and `PanelType` has no equivalent. Priced at zero, a job would appear to form itself more cheaply the more of its own kit it used, which is exactly backwards from why a yard buys formwork. So `ownedQuantityExcluded` reports the quantity left out and a caveat says why. **A quote beats a rule of thumb**, for the same reason: `rentalPerUnitPerMonth` overrides `purchasePerUnit × rentalPercentPerMonth` wherever both are stated, because the percentage is how the trade *talks* about hire and the flat figure is what somebody actually quoted.

**`CostGap` is per line rather than one "incomplete" flag, because the gaps have different remedies.** A missing rate is a table somebody fills in; a hire percentage with no list price under it is a rate for a figure the project never stated (and `set_formwork_settings` refuses it outright rather than storing something that would price nothing); a consumable with no purchase price is a different missing cell. The gap worth the most is **`hired-but-never-struck`**, which is a real cost with no way to price it: a tie carries a catalog id and is standard stock, so `bomSupply` puts it on the hired side, and `strikeTargetForPartKind` returns nothing for a tie because it is cut off inside the wall. Neither answer is wrong alone. Reported rather than dropped, because a bill missing every tie in a job still totals cleanly.

**It reaches every surface a bill reaches, in the same words**, and the words are the substance. `ProjectFormwork.cost` is present only where rates are recorded; `projectFormworkCaveats` leads a priced takeoff with what the price is *not*. `bom-csv.ts` splices the money columns and omits them entirely without a rate table — a column of blanks headed "Hire cost" is the most confident wrong answer that file could give. The takeoff panel gets a Cost section. Both AI reads carry the per-line figures and a `cost` block whose `excludes` array is not boilerplate: labour (normally the largest cost of forming a job), transport and craneage, finance and preliminaries, and the project's own owned stock. Both AI *writes* carry `rates` through core's shared `settings-patch.ts`, and both system prompts now say that a rate is never assumed, that `daysCharged` is what reconciles with an invoice, and that a total is a floor whenever `complete` is false. The editor panel's "Rates" group closes the parity the other direction — without it a model could price a job the user could not.

**Three deviations from the shape this was specified as, each for a reason worth recording.** `basis` was dropped from the per-part rate: the basis is a property of the *line*, not the part, because `bomSupply` has already decided which of a panel type's 26 are owned, hired or consumed, and a stated `basis: 'purchase'` against a panel the bill hires would be a second answer to a settled question. A hired panel drilled for this pour needs both a hire charge and a recharge, so a part carries what it is worth and what it costs to hold, and the bill decides which applies. `minHireDays` moved from the part to the group, as above. And `cleaningPerUse` was dropped as outside the chosen hire-only scope — it is a per-return charge and belongs with labour.

Covered by 25 tests in `cost.test.ts`, 12 in core's `settings.test.ts` over `mergeFormworkRates` and the resolver, 13 in `settings-patch.test.ts` over the shared write contract and its two refusals, 7 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 6 in `solve-project.test.ts` plus 2 more caveat tests, 9 in `use-formwork-settings.test.ts`, 6 in `chat-ai-project-formwork.test.ts` and 6 in the MCP suite. Two mutations were tried on the surfaces rather than the engine, because that is where a wrong join is invisible: pinning the per-line lookup to `lines[0]` and charging the whole quantity instead of the hired remainder each killed a test on both the chat surface and in `cost.test.ts`.

**The tie fixture is worth recording, because guessing at it twice is what the probe was for.** A test asserting `hired-but-never-struck` needs a rate against the tie the bill *actually* carries, and a steel-panel wall bills `doka-framax-tie-588681000` (the combination nut for one-sided tying) rather than `doka-framax-tie-dw15` (the rod, which is cut to length). Keyed to the wrong id the test still passes its own assertion shape and reports `no-rate` instead — the same failure the CSV tests hit earlier by keying a map on a different `BomLine` array than the producer used.

## What landed: the day the pour happens

**The programme — `systems/formwork/schedule.ts`, `pourAt` on the assembly, and two lead times on the project.** The bill could say what a job needs, whose rack it comes off, how long it is held and what holding it costs. It could not say *when*, which is the field a delivery note has at the top and the one a following trade actually waits on. This adds it, and it adds almost no arithmetic: every date it prints is a pour date somebody stated plus or minus a period `hire.ts` had already solved.

**A date is stated and a period is derived, and that asymmetry is the whole design.** A real scheduler computes a pour date from dependencies, float and a resource calendar; nothing in this model has any of the three. So the alternative to a stated date was a *derived* one — off `castOrder`, or off the order the shutters were built — and that would have been worse than no date at all: a programme the project never agreed to, printed in the same panel as geometry that genuinely is derived and carrying exactly the same authority. Somebody books a crane against it. So `pourAt` is an input, an undated pour gets **no dates**, and `unscheduled` counts them, because a window over 3 of 40 pours is a true statement about 3 pours and a wrong one about the job.

**The date belongs to the pour, not to the element, and this is what the assembly node was for.** A 9 m wall cast in three lifts is three dates a week apart; a field on the wall could only ever be one of them, and whichever it was would be wrong about the other two by a fortnight. `FormworkAssemblyNode` already carries `segmentIndex`/`liftIndex` precisely because a pour unit is the thing a shutter belongs to, so `pourAt` sits there — and the two *lead times* sit on the project, because erecting a shutter takes as long as this gang takes and a different figure per wall would be a claim about the walls rather than about the crew.

**Absent is the answer, for the third time and the rates' reason exactly.** `curing` is undefaulted because the striking tables print their own conservative column, so silence has a published answer. A rate is undefaulted because there is no conservative direction at all — guess low and the job under-bids, high and it loses the tender. A lead time is the rate's case: there is no table behind "how long before the pour does this yard want the plant", and a default of **zero** would say the shutter appears on the morning of the pour, which is the one answer that is certainly wrong. So with no lead time recorded there is no delivery date on the takeoff, only a pour and a strike.

**Calendar days, not working days, and that is a decision rather than a simplification.** Everything these figures are added to is calendar: a hire is charged over a weekend, and a striking period is either calendar time or an accumulator over qualifying hours — neither is a working week. Skipping weekends would need a working calendar and a holiday list nothing in this model carries, and the result would be a delivery date the hire invoice disagrees with. Rounding is **up** to the day everywhere, for the one reason that matters: ACI's 12 hours on a vertical form becomes one day, because rounding down prints a strike date before the code's own period has elapsed, and that is the only error in this module that could hurt somebody.

**ACI's clock is not a calendar, and the date says so instead of converting.** A BS 8110 period is calendar time — add it to a date and the answer is a date. ACI's is an accumulator over hours above 10 °C, so 4 days of qualifying time is 4 days in a warm July and a fortnight in a cold spring. There is no weather here, so under ACI every strike date is the **earliest** the form could come off, `earliestOnly` carries it, and the CSV prints the label before any of the dates rather than after them. Presenting it as *the* date is how a reader takes a cold-spring programme off a summer calculation and strikes a slab early.

**`strikeAt` is the last strike, not the first, and `scheduleOccupancyDays` is not `bomHire.longestHours`.** Both distinctions are the same mistake in two places. A slab's deck comes off days before its props, and both are in `strikes` — collapsing them to the earlier one shows a set as available while it is still holding a floor up, so `strikeAt` is the day the *set* comes free. And the occupancy figure is arrival-to-release across every pour in the scope, where `longestHours` is how long one pour holds its plant: a set used on five pours a week apart is held two days each time and on site for five weeks. Both are true and only the second is what a yard invoices, so the panel prints them as separate rows with separate captions.

**One sort rule, in core, because the obvious version of it is wrong.** Ordering the programme with a `'~'` sentinel for the undated pours looks equivalent to partitioning them and is not: `localeCompare` treats punctuation as ignorable, so the sentinel collates *before* a date and the undated pours head the programme as though they began the job. `scheduleInPourOrder` partitions datedness explicitly and every reader calls it, since a CSV that disagreed with the panel above it about the order of a programme is a disagreement about the job.

**The dates go in the CSV's preamble rather than in a column, and that follows from what a bill line is.** A line is per catalog id at one condition and therefore spans pours — the same panel type serves three lifts — so a date against it would silently name one of them. An undated pour is an `INCOMPLETE` row rather than a blank cell in a sequence, for the reason an unknown weight is a blank rather than a zero.

**The write is four surfaces over one contract, and the browser's own date control is the reason there is a contract.** `schedule-patch.ts` owns it: `applyPourDatePatch` validates, `isCalendarDate` refuses `2026-02-30`, and `null` deletes the key. That check cannot be left to the input: a `type="date"` control emits `2026-02-30` for a February somebody overtyped, the schema's regex accepts it, and `Date.UTC` reads it back as **1 March** — so every date derived from it is a day out while the field still shows what was typed. The inspector shows the refusal rather than swallowing it, since a box that silently keeps a value nothing was stored for is worse than one that says why. The four surfaces are the assembly inspector's Programme group, `set_pour_date` on both AI surfaces, and the settings panel's two lead-time fields — whose own note points the user *away*, to the shutter, because the panel that holds the lead times is the first place anybody will look for the dates.

**`OptionalNumberField.assumed` became optional in the same change, and the omission is a claim.** Naming a conservative figure and naming none are different statements: with `assumed` absent the field reads "not stated" and the "Assumed X." hint is suppressed entirely, rather than printing a sentence about a fallback that does not exist. Two fields had been passing `assumed="not stated"` as a workaround (poker depth, slump) and are now honest omissions.

Covered by 22 tests in core's new `schedule.test.ts`, 7 in `schedule-patch.test.ts`, 5 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 6 in `solve-project.test.ts`, 5 in core's `settings.test.ts`, 6 in `chat-ai-project-formwork.test.ts` and 6 in the MCP suite. Two assertions are worth naming because neither surface could make the other's: the chat suite checks `schedule.daysOnSite > hire.longestDaysHeld` on a project with lead times, which is the numeric form of "the leads are in the occupancy and in neither period"; and both write suites assert the undated pours sort **last**, which is the `localeCompare` trap above and the one thing a sentinel implementation would pass every other test with.

## What landed: how many sets the job needs

**The set count — `systems/formwork/sets.ts`.** A bill is not an order, and the difference is reuse. Every quantity in a takeoff is what passes through the job; what somebody buys is what stands *at the same time*. The same 100 panels serving twenty sequential pours is one purchase and serving twenty simultaneous ones is twenty, which is a 20× difference in what a yard commits to off an identical bill. That is the economics the whole trade runs on and it is the last question this feature could not answer.

**The dates made it expressible and nothing else here is new arithmetic.** `supply.ts` has carried the caveat since it was written — two scopes' owned figures are not additive because the same panels serve the next pour once stripped — and `schedule.ts` gave every dated pour an `erectAt`→`releaseAt` interval. The maximum number of overlapping intervals on any day *is* the count. The plan asks for a maximum clique over an interval graph, which is the same thing said harder: on intervals it is a linear sweep, not a graph problem. No geometry is solved here and no date is invented.

**The refusal is the module's centre, not a guard on it.** Every other gap in this feature is visible in its own output: an unpriced bill has no money in it, an undated project has no calendar. A set count off a partial programme is a **plausible small number** — 3 dated pours out of 40 barely overlap, so the sweep reports a peak of one set, and one set is what a reader orders. So below `SET_COUNT_COVERAGE_THRESHOLD = 0.9` there is **no count at all**, and above it the count is a **floor** with the shortfall named, because an undated pour can only add to an overlap and never subtract from one. This file has made the absent-is-the-answer decision four times before (`notChecked`, `complete`, absent rates, absent dates); this is the first time the absence had to be *explained*, because it is the only one where the input is visibly present.

**The interval is per *item*, not per pour, and that is the bug worth having found.** The first version gave each pour one interval and applied it to everything in that pour — which quietly falsifies the module's own central claim. A deck comes off in 4 days and the props under it stay 10, so their peaks fall on different days and there is no single moment at which the job is at maximum. One interval per pour puts both peaks on the same day and reports a job needing both sets at once. So `PourQuantities.quantities[]` carries its own `StrikeTarget` and `intervalFor(window, target)` derives that item's own hold from the pour's own strike dates — recovered from the dates the programme printed rather than read from settings, so a per-target release cannot disagree with the release on screen.

**Per catalog id, and per kind as a *sum* of those peaks rather than a second sweep.** Two panel types peaking a fortnight apart still need both peaks' worth of rack, because a 2.4 m panel does not cover for a 1.2 m one. Sweeping the kind would report the larger of the two and under-order. So `kinds` is explicitly a sum and every surface says so, since "rack: 180 panels" is the figure a reader will quote.

**The interval ends the day *before* `releaseAt`, and the divergence from `scheduleOccupancyDays` is deliberate.** `releaseAt` is documented as the day the set is available again, so counting it as occupied double-books it and back-to-back pours read as needing two sets when they share one. `scheduleOccupancyDays` counts that day because it is what a yard invoices; this counts when a gang can refit. The consequence — that back-to-back pours are shown sharing one set with no slack for striking, cleaning and refitting, which no gang does in a day — is named in the caveats on all four surfaces rather than absorbed into the arithmetic as a fudge factor.

**Only returnable lines are poolable, and the rule was extracted rather than restated.** `isReturnableLine` came out of `supply.ts` because a second copy of it would be a second thing to fix: a cut board reported as reused would say one board serves five pours. And the per-pour quantities come from each shutter's own `bomLines`, never from the project bill — a bill line is one catalog id across every pour in scope, so it has no pour to belong to, and a sweep over it would find one interval covering the whole job.

**Two smaller decisions that only look like details.** A peak tie resolves to the **earliest** day, because a peak is a procurement date and the reader is asking when the stock has to be on site. And only interval *start* days can raise a total, so the sweep examines starts rather than calendar days — linear in pours rather than in a programme that may span years.

**`reuseFactor === 1` for everything is said out loud rather than left as two matching numbers.** A single pour, or a programme with no overlap, means the count *is* the bill. Two identical figures on different panels with no sentence between them is how a reader concludes one of them is broken.

**It reaches all four surfaces, and the refusal reaches them as data.** `ProjectFormwork.sets`, `projectFormworkCaveats`, the takeoff panel's "Most needed at once" section, the CSV's `MOST NEEDED AT ONCE` preamble block, and `sets` on both AI reads with `noSetCountBecause` where there is none. That last field is the one shape on the AI surfaces a model would otherwise report as a bug: an absent `cost` sits beside a bill with no money in it and an absent `schedule` beside a project with no dates, but an absent `sets` beside a *present* `schedule` looks like a tool that failed. Four prohibitions went into both tool descriptions — quote `sets` and never `bom` when asked what to buy, `reuses` is what decides owning against hiring, `rack` is a sum rather than a sweep, and never subtract a peak from a bill quantity because the difference is not a quantity of anything. **The second of those four was wrong and has since been corrected on both surfaces:** `reuses` is *not* the buy-or-hire argument, because hire is charged per unit per month, so a set fitted eight times inside one month costs exactly what a set fitted once inside it does. Both descriptions now point at `acquire` for that question and say so in as many words. It is worth leaving the error recorded, because it is the trade's own rule of thumb ("buy it if you use it more than five times") and it was persuasive enough to be written into two tool descriptions before the arithmetic was done.

Covered by 16 tests in core's new `sets.test.ts`, 7 in `solve-project.test.ts`, 6 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 4 in the MCP suite and 4 in `chat-ai-project-formwork.test.ts`. The two assertions that earned their place are the ones keyed on the *physics* rather than the output shape: a set freed on Thursday can be refitted on Thursday (which is what caught the off-by-one), and a deck is reused where its props are not (which is what caught the per-pour interval). A third holds the bill still and moves only the dates — the same two walls poured a fortnight apart and poured on one day, off an identical bill, produce a half-bill order and a whole-bill one, which is the entire claim of the module in one test.

## What landed: what the job is short of, and whether to own it

**`core/systems/formwork/acquire.ts`, and the owned stock priced in `cost.ts`.** The set count said how many stand at once. This says what the yard does not have, when it has to have it, and what the two courses cost — and it corrects a figure this feature has been publishing since the supply split.

**A shortfall is the peak against the rack, never the bill against the rack.** `bomSupply` splits the *bill*: a yard owning 100 panels against a bill of 400 is told it hires 300. On four sequential pours that is wrong by a factor of three, because the same 100 panels serve all four and the yard hires nothing. The split has carried that caveat since it was written and named the set count as what would fix it; this is the fix. The consequence is a number a reader may already have quoted, so it is said outright on every surface: a project can carry a large hired quantity in its bill and have nothing at all to acquire. Both figures are right and neither is the other, which is also why the acquisition block sits in the CSV *preamble* rather than as two more columns beside `To hire` — in adjacent columns the reader takes the difference, and the difference is not a quantity of anything.

**Buy-or-hire is not about reuse, and the trade's own rule of thumb is the thing to argue with.** "Buy it if you use it more than five times" is about uses, and uses do not enter this comparison at all: hire is charged per unit per month, so a set fitted eight times inside one month costs exactly what a set fitted once inside it does. Both courses scale identically with quantity, so the ratio between them depends on one thing only — how long the item is held. At 3 % of new value a month, the middle of the band a hire desk quotes, a purchase needs some 33 months of continuous hire to pay for itself, and almost no single job holds a set that long. So the honest output is that **hire wins on nearly any one job**, and owning is justified by a succession of them.

That is why the reported figure is `paybackJobs = purchaseCost / hireCost` rather than a break-even number of uses, and why every surface is instructed to quote it *instead of* the verdict. "Hire — but owning pays back over 2.1 jobs like this" is a purchase for a yard with three more booked and a hire for one with none. The decision is about an order book this model cannot see; the model's job is to say exactly where the line falls. The verdict itself carries a ±10 % `MARGINAL_BAND`, because a model with no residual value in it should not call a decision it is within a tenth of.

**What makes the comparison possible is the span, and the span is not the sum of the strike periods.** `SetPeak` grew `committedFrom`, `committedTo`, `committedDays` and `fittedUnitDays`. A set used on five pours a week apart is held two days each time and *on site* for five weeks; the yard invoices the five weeks. Costing a hire off the ten days is the single easiest way to under-price a reused set by 70 %, and it is also the way to make buying look absurd. `utilisation` — `fittedUnitDays / (peakQuantity × committedDays)` — is the ratio those two figures make, and below about 0.5 it says the hire is paying for plant standing idle, which is a programme with gaps in it rather than a fault in the design.

**Owned stock is now charged, and it is charged as an internal hire rather than amortised.** `cost.ts` had excluded owned parts on the grounds that pricing a sunk asset needs a life in uses nothing in the catalog carries. That gap is real and is still open — `SheetStock.expectedReuses` exists, `PanelType` has no equivalent, and `SetPeak.reuseFactor` does **not** close it, because how many times this job fits a panel says nothing about how many fittings the panel has left. But excluding owned stock entirely made *owning formwork look free*, which is the more misleading of the two errors. So an owned part is charged at what the project itself says hiring one costs, for the period the line is held: no invented figure, no life, just the project's stated rate against a span this engine already derived. No minimum period applies, because that is a term of an agreement with a hire company and a yard does not fine itself for striking early. It is deliberately outside `totalCost` — a tender needs the cash and an internal costing needs both, and one total would be neither — and every surface is told never to add the two.

`ownedQuantityExcluded` was redefined in the same change to mean only the owned parts that *still* cannot be charged, so a zero now means the recharge is complete rather than that the yard owns nothing. Two fields that both meant "owned" and disagreed about what they measured would be the next bug.

**It fires as an invariant, which is the surface the set count never reached.** `SET_COUNT_SHORTAGE` had been in the permanent `notChecked` list since phase 11, with its reason narrowing twice. It now fires as a warning naming the item, the peak, the day, the rack and both remedies — acquire the difference, or move one of the overlapping pours out of the overlap. It takes the solve's own acquisition as evidence rather than deriving one, for the same reason the packs and the envelopes are passed in: a second set count could disagree with the takeoff about how many panels are short, and a validator that re-derives its own evidence is a validator that can contradict the screen. Its `elementIds` come from an `elementIdByPourId` map built in `packages/nodes`, because a shortage names *pours* and a pour id is an assembly id, which core's validator never sees — the same layer reason `bomHire`'s `targetsByMark` has. And its absence is now conditional rather than permanent, with three distinguishable causes: no pour dated, no rack recorded, or a programme too partial to sweep.

**Five surfaces, and the CSV was the one that found a bug.** `ProjectFormwork.acquisition`, `acquireCaveats`, the takeoff panel's "To acquire" section, the CSV's `TO ACQUIRE` preamble block, both AI reads with `noAcquisitionBecause`, and the validation finding on the panel and both AI validate calls. The CSV block's own summary row — `Whole shortfall, hired against bought` — was pushed unquoted and split into extra columns, which is exactly the RFC 4180 failure `bom-csv.ts` exists to prevent, caught by a test written after the code. The `On site by` cell is left blank where the shortfall is zero, because a date beside a zero reads as a delivery somebody has to make. And the two total figures are never differenced, because buying instead of hiring is not a saving of the difference; it is a different decision with a different balance sheet.

Covered by 20 tests in core's new `acquire.test.ts`, 6 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 6 in `validate-project.test.ts`, 2 in `takeoff.test.ts`, plus the `cost.test.ts` and `sets.test.ts` additions for the recharge and the span. Two of them earned their place. One holds the bill still and moves only the rack: the *same* scene, sequential and concurrent, is short of nothing and short of three — which is the module's whole claim in one assertion. The other is the one that failed on my own premise: a rack holding only panels leaves the ties, walers and props short, so the shortage fired on a scene the test called covered, and the fix was to size the rack off the programme's own peaks per catalog id rather than by hand. A rack is per item, and a test that forgets that is a test asserting the wrong scene.

## What landed: one write contract, three surfaces

**`core/systems/formwork/settings-patch.ts`, and the two MCP tools over it.** The visible half is that an outside agent can now state the pour: `set_formwork_settings` and `inspect_formwork_settings` are on MCP, so the surface that could already quote a bill can say what the bill is designed to. The half worth recording is why the module exists at all.

**The reads shipped alone, and that left MCP quoting a design nobody could correct.** Every figure the two read tools returned was solved against DIN's fastest covered rise rate at its own reference temperature, because those are the shipped conservative defaults and nothing on this surface could say otherwise. An agent could tell a yard what to order, and had no way to record the 2 m/h the job actually pours at or the 5 °C it cures at — the second of which is the input the entire hire period hangs off. That is the narrower half of the feature presented as the whole of it.

**Writing the pair the obvious way would have copied ~250 lines out of the chat tools, and the copy is the failure.** Eight zod group schemas, four catalog id lists, the part and stock validators, `toPatch`, the merge loop and both tool descriptions. Two AI surfaces that disagree about what `null` means, or about which catalog ids are real, disagree about whether the design report says "assumed" or "project" — which is the single distinction the settings node exists to carry. It is the same parity hazard the settings pair was originally built to close between the panel and the chat tool, arriving one layer further out.

**The descriptions are as much of the contract as the bounds, which is the part a second author would not reproduce.** They are the only place a model is told that a colder mix raises the pressure while a colder cure lengthens the hold, that `curing.surfaceTemperatureC` is not `placement.concreteTemperatureC`, or that a hallucinated part id falls back silently rather than failing. A warning present in one copy and absent in the other is a wrong strike time on whichever surface lacks it, reported with the same confidence as a right one.

**`null` is a third state, and it is the reason this is not the node's own schema.** The node has two states per field — stated, or absent. An agent needs three: state it, leave it alone, hand it back. Without the third a model can set a figure and never retract it, so every assumption in a project decays into a claim over a conversation. So each field is `.nullable().optional()` derived over the node's own bounds (which are therefore kept — `surfaceTemperatureC: 999` is refused by the patch schema, not by a hand-written duplicate of the range), `null` becomes the `undefined` the merge helpers spell as "unstate", and an absent key is left alone.

**It returns writes rather than performing them, which is what keeps it in core.** The two callers apply differently — the chat tools mutate a plain server-side graph, MCP goes through the store's `updateNode` — and both already honour the same delete semantics: an explicitly-`undefined` key *removes* the key rather than storing it, because an optional field's absence is what encodes a mode. The panel's `clearFormworkSettings()` has depended on that since it shipped. A shared *writer* would have to know about a store, which is the wrong side of this package's boundary. Refusals are values for the same reason: "no beamId peri-h20, pick one of …" is a sentence a model can act on, where a thrown error arrives as a failed call with no catalog in it.

**Extracting it found a real bug in the chat tool.** `set_formwork_settings` merged the binder onto `node.concrete` as it stood *before* the call, so a single call stating both `concrete` and `cement` discarded the concrete merge — a model that set the unit weight and the retarder in one breath kept only the retarder. The core version chains the binder through the writes it has already produced, and `settings-patch.test.ts` asserts it directly. Nothing on either surface would have shown this: both fields were accepted, the reply said both were set, and the figure that went missing was one the project had merely stated less recently.

**One thing MCP needs that the panel does not.** The MCP writer needs **no dirty sweep**: the panel's writer marks every assembly in the scene, because a design input lives outside the shutters it sizes and an incremental `updateNode` reaches only the edited node and its parent — without it the report re-reads the new pour while the 3D shutters keep the spacings they were built with. `publishLiveSceneSnapshot` sends the whole graph for a `setScene` rebuild, so there is nothing incremental to mark, and `markDirty` is not on the MCP bridge at all: there is no renderer in that process to notify.

**The node is parented to the site or the loader sweeps it**, the same failure the chat path documents: an orphan survives the reply and vanishes on reload, the pour silently reverting to the shipped defaults with nothing to show it ever changed. A siteless scene is refused rather than given one. MCP can parse the node rather than hand-building it — `FormworkProjectSettingsNode.parse({ parentId })` self-generates the id — where the chat tools must hand-build, because `generateId` reads a browser-side counter.

**The agent guide had to carry the write as well, for the same reason it carries the reads.** An MCP host gives a server no system prompt, so the reasoning `SYSTEM_PROMPT` states on the chat side lives in `pascal://agent-guide` — and a guide that says how to *read* a bill and not that the pour behind it is settable leaves a model presenting an assumed 7 m/h at 20 °C as a figure the job agreed to. Three lines went in: read the settings before quoting any spacing, write them rather than guessing, and the two temperatures are different fields. The guide's formwork section had no test at all, which is why the omission was possible; it has one now.

Covered by 25 tests in core's new `settings-patch.test.ts`, 10 in the MCP formwork suite and 1 over the agent guide. The 38 tests in `chat-ai-formwork-settings.test.ts` pass **unchanged** against the extracted module, which is the assertion that matters most here: the shared contract is the behaviour that shipped, not a reinterpretation of it.

## What landed: the mark as a write key

**`core/systems/formwork/part-patch.ts`, `nodes/src/formwork-assembly/parts-report.ts`, and the two MCP tools over them.** An outside agent can now read a shutter part by part and record a decision about one of those parts: `inspect_formwork_parts` and `set_formwork_part` are on MCP. The interesting half is not the tools, which are thin, but what made this write different from every other one on this surface.

**It is the first write whose input cannot be validated from a schema.** Every other MCP write states something the node already has a field for, and the bounds on that field are the whole check. A mark is not that: `P-A-1-01800` names a position in a *solved* layout — face A, first course, 1800 mm along — and nothing in the scene graph carries it. So `set_formwork_part` runs `solveShuttersForHost` on a **write** path, which nothing else here does, and refuses a mark the solve does not produce.

**Resolving it is not politeness, because writing it blind fails silently and permanently.** `partOverrides` is keyed by mark and accepts any string, so a mark the model misremembered writes cleanly, reports "ok", and then sits in the project as a *stale edit* — reported by every later read as somebody's forgotten decision about a part that was never edited, for the rest of the job. The override is not cleared automatically on purpose (a wall shortened below a panel and lengthened again should get its decisions back), so nothing downstream will ever tidy it up.

**Two refusals, not one, and core owns both so the two surfaces say the same words.** A wrong mark on a shuttered wall is a lookup to redo — "call `inspect_formwork_parts` for the marks this shutter actually has (94 parts)". An element nobody has formed needs other calls first — "call `set_element_construction` then `attach_formwork`" for the read, "call `attach_formwork` first" for the write. Merged into one message, whichever case the agent is actually in, it is sent to the wrong remedy.

**The read is refused rather than answered empty, for the same reason the project bill names its unshuttered elements.** `formworkPartsReport` returns `undefined` for an unformed element, not a report with no parts in it, because a bill of nothing reads as an element that needs *nothing* — which is the exact opposite of an element awaiting a shutter, and indistinguishable from it in a reply.

**What would have diverged between the two surfaces is the shape, not the numbers — which is why the report is a shared type.** Both call the same solver, so no figure can disagree. The shape can, silently: `partCount` following the `kind` filter is a right number to a different question, and the model quotes it as the shutter's; `duplicateMarks` flattened across lifts is a clash list that is always full on a correctly built three-lift wall, because a mark's station and elevation are measured *within* its own pour unit, and a clash list that is always full is one nobody reads. The rounding site is shared for the same reason — two surfaces rounding at different places report one shutter working at 0.87 and at 0.8746, which a user reading both takes for two designs.

**The report sits in `packages/nodes` and the patch in core, and the split is the layer boundary rather than taste.** The read needs the solve, which lives behind the geometry builders; core has no access to it and should not. The write needs the catalog and the merge, which are core's. So the mark is resolved by each surface against its own live solve, and both then call one `applyFormworkPartPatch` with the assembly's current overrides — which returns the new overrides rather than writing them, because the chat tools mutate a plain server-side graph and MCP goes through the store's `updateNode`.

**The catalog id is checked even though a bad one does not fail loudly** — which is exactly why. The design chain falls back to its own default part, so a hallucinated `peri-h20` would leave the project believing it had specified a beam while every span was solved against another. One flat `isStockableCatalogId` check rather than a per-kind list: the question is only whether the id names a real product, and a tighter check would reject the legitimate case of a column form standing in for a wall panel on a blade.

**`omitted: false` clears rather than stores a false.** `mergeFormworkPartOverride` deletes `undefined | false | ''` and drops a mark whose record has emptied, so putting a part back on the order leaves no `{}` behind to be reported as a stale edit later. The reply says "back on the order" rather than "note recorded" over a field that was just emptied — the one sentence that would leave a user believing the drawing carries a reason it does not.

**Extracting it deleted the chat tools' local copies rather than adding a second one.** `CATALOG_IDS`, `toPatch` and the local `coverageCaveat` are gone from `chat-ai.ts`, and `shutterMismatch` is now one line over the shared `formworkCoverageCaveat`. The previous section of this file recorded `CATALOG_IDS` and `toPatch` as *staying* local because `set_formwork_part` used them; that is what closed here.

**Two guide lines went in**, for the reason the settings pair's three did: an MCP host gives a server no system prompt, so what the mark is *for* has nowhere else to be said. A mark is a stable quotable handle and the only way to name a part to the write; a substitution will not line up with another system's tie holes; and one element is rarely the scope a yard is asking about, so anything to be ordered goes through `inspect_project_formwork`.

Covered by 9 tests in core's new `part-patch.test.ts`, 12 in nodes' new `parts-report.test.ts`, 11 in the MCP formwork suite and 1 over the agent guide. The 16 tests in `chat-ai-formwork-parts.test.ts` and the 15 in `chat-ai-formwork-reattach.test.ts` pass **unchanged** against the extracted modules, which is again the assertion that matters most: the shared contract is the behaviour that shipped.

## What landed: the surface closes, and a refusal that pointed nowhere

**`core/systems/formwork/construction-patch.ts`, and the last five MCP formwork tools over it and `pour-patch.ts`.** The pour pair, `set_pour_limits` and `attach_formwork`, and the three that closed the surface, `list_castable_elements`, `set_element_construction` and `inspect_pour_units`. That brought MCP and the editor's AI to the same eleven, which `set_pour_date` has since made twelve.

**The three thin reads stopped being deferrable the moment a refusal named one of them.** `noFormworkTypeSet` — core's refusal, shared by both surfaces since the parts pair — tells the agent to "call `set_element_construction` first", and on MCP there was no such tool. An agent handed a remedy it cannot call does not stop: it guesses a formwork system, and a guessed system produces a complete, plausible, entirely wrong bill. So these three were pulled into the pour pair's change rather than kept as the cheap tail of the phase. A refusal that points nowhere is worse than no refusal, because it reads as a workflow.

**Porting the chat tools onto the shared module was the work, and it surfaced three divergences that were already live.** None of them was introduced here; all three were differences between what a user's own AI could do and what an agent could, which is the class of bug the shared modules exist to prevent, found only because the second implementation forced a comparison:

- `set_element_construction` on chat accepted **7 of the 15** construction fields. `pourId`, `againstEarthSide`, `exposureClass` and `kickerMode` were unreachable, and there was no way to *unstate* anything at all — so a tie spacing set once could not be handed back to the solver.
- After naming a formwork system it replied `ok` and said nothing about building anything. That is this write's most likely failure and it is a *success*: a correct `formworkType`, reported fine, no attach behind it, and a project that believes it specified a steel-panel wall while holding no shutter, no bill and nothing on screen. Both surfaces now derive `formingTurnedOn` — true when this call named a real system on an element that had none — and combine it with a zero shutter count into one sentence naming the next call.
- `inspect_pour_units` emitted raw enum cut reasons (`MAX_POUR_VOLUME`) and no shutter context. Both now emit `POUR_CUT_REASON_LABELS`, because an MCP host has no system prompt to translate an enum with, and both carry `pourUnitCount`, `shutterCount`, `totalVolumeCuM` and the same `coverageCaveat` the parts read and the limit write already used.

**The slab-only fields are refused rather than dropped, and the whole call with them.** `edgeFaceCount` and `soffitHeightAboveSupport` are declared on `SlabNode` alone while the other thirteen come from the shared `ShutteringFields`/`CastableFields`, so the schema cannot police them and `applyConstructionPatch` has to. Refused, because a silently dropped field is a decision the user believes was recorded; and refused *entirely*, because a partial write leaves the agent's own account of what it did wrong in a way nothing later contradicts.

**`null` unstates, an absent key leaves alone, and the two cannot be collapsed** — the same three-state contract `pour-patch.ts` already had, for the same reason: an unstated tie spacing is what encodes "solve this from the pressure envelope", so stored as a `null` it would read as a stated figure of nothing. Both callers spell the unstate as an explicit `undefined` and apply it as a deletion.

**`castableElementSummary` is shared because the *omissions* carry meaning.** A field visible to one AI and missing from the other is a field the second one asks the user to restate — which is how a user comes to believe the agent lost their answer. So the ~30 lines of hand-rolled per-kind mapping in `chat-ai.ts` are gone, replaced by the same function MCP calls, and the MCP output schema passes the summary through as a record rather than restating its fields: a restated list is the shape that drifts.

**Per-unit volume and the total are separate fields, and the description forbids adding them.** `volumeCuM` per unit is what one delivery has to supply before the first concrete placed reaches initial set — the batch-plant constraint. `totalVolumeCuM` is the concrete order. Added together the units make a 3-lift wall read as pourable in one go, which is the arithmetic that produces a cold joint.

**A lift joint has no cut reason, and that is correct rather than missing.** The test that failed here asserted `MAX_LIFT_HEIGHT` on a lift-split wall's `endCut`. `startCut`/`endCut` are cuts along the *centreline*; a lift joint is horizontal, so a three-lift wall has none, and the elevations plus `limits.maxLiftHeight` are what answer "why is there a joint at 3 m". `MAX_LIFT_HEIGHT` exists in `PourCutReason` for the lift *joint* nodes rather than for a segment boundary. The assertion was wrong about the engine, not the reverse — recorded because it is an easy conclusion to reach twice.

**Nine guide lines went in**, for the reason the settings pair's three and the parts pair's two did: an MCP host gives a server no system prompt. Two of them carry the failures that produce no error at all — read `unshuttered` before quoting a total, and follow a `formworkType` with an attach — since those are the two ways this surface returns a complete and wrong answer.

The 126 tests then in `apps/editor/lib` (138 now) passed **unchanged** against the extracted module, which is again the assertion that matters: the shared contract is the behaviour that shipped, widened where the chat surface was narrower than core's own schema.

## What landed: what has to happen before what, and which pour is free to move

**`core/systems/formwork/sequence.ts` and `resequence.ts`, on all four surfaces.** The shortage finding has been naming the pours whose overlap creates each shortfall and offering "move one of them" as a remedy it could not evaluate, in those words, since the acquisition shipped. This evaluates it. It is the first output in the feature that proposes a change to the project rather than describing one, and everything below follows from being careful about that.

**Precedence needed no schema, because the project has stated it three times over.** No field was added anywhere:

- **`liftIndex`** — lift N's formwork bears on lift N−1, so N cannot be erected until N−1 is *struck*. Physical, never optional, and therefore the only precedence that exists at all on a job nobody has sequenced. Per element *segment* rather than per element, and over consecutive *present* lifts rather than N−1 exactly, because an unformed lift in the middle removes the shutter and not the bearing.
- **`castOrder`** — an explicit integer per element, already cycle-checked by `CAST_ORDER_CYCLE`.
- **`pourId`** — elements cast in one operation. Simultaneity is not an edge: they contract to one *node*, since a node cannot precede itself, and a monolithic pour therefore moves whole.

An unsequenced pair is **concurrent**, which is the reading `castOrder`'s own schema comment already commits to. It is reported rather than assumed: `nothing-sequenced` and `unsequenced` are gaps a reader sees, because every figure downstream is true of that graph rather than of the programme somebody has in their head.

**Float is local, and refusing a forward pass is the same judgement `schedule.ts` already made.** A classical CPM pass starts from a project start date and computes every pour's early start, which is an entire invented programme printed beside geometry that is genuinely derived — the exact thing `formworkSchedule` refuses when it declines to derive a pour date. So each pour's window is bounded by the **stated** dates of its own neighbours, plus the programme's own span (`firstErectAt`…`lastReleaseAt`) for a pour with no neighbour on one side. One pass over the edges, no topological order, no derived calendar.

**A lift edge waits on a strike; a cast-order edge waits on a pour** — and that difference is days rather than a nicety. The lift above cannot be erected until the lift below is struck, so its earliest is `predecessor.strikeDay + leadDays`; a cast order only says which concrete goes first, so its earliest is `predecessor.pourDay`. The cast-order test is also not strict, because at day resolution two pours in stated order may share a day and requiring one between them would invent a constraint the calendar cannot see.

**Every window carries the pour's own shape with it.** A move takes its delivery and its release along, so the bounds are `windowStart + leadDays` and `windowEnd − tailDays`. Without those two terms the window would happily propose a move whose plant arrives before the job starts.

**"Pinned", never "critical path".** A CPM critical path is a path through a derived programme; the zero-float pours here are pours pinned by their neighbours' *stated* dates, which is a different and weaker claim — adding a date elsewhere can change it. The word appears in the type (`pinned`), in the caveats, in the panel, in the CSV's block header and in the tool descriptions, because "critical path" is what a reader will call it anyway and the export outlives the panel it came from.

**Float is not a pool, and that is a caveat rather than a footnote.** Two concurrent pours with a week each do not have two weeks between them: the second one's window was measured against the first one's stated date, which the first one just left. One move, then re-solve. This is also why `resequence.ts` never proposes two moves — a list of five reads as a plan.

**Negative float is reported rather than clamped.** Where the stated dates already break a stated dependency, `conflicts` names the pair and the float goes negative: how many days the programme is infeasible by. The panel prints `n d late already` for exactly that case, because a clamped `0` would read as a tight-but-workable pour.

**`allowanceDays: null` and `0` are opposite claims, and every surface keeps them apart.** Nothing bounds this pour, versus this pour cannot move. Both AI blocks carry `?? null` for that reason and the MCP description says so outright, since in JSON the two look alike and a model reading a fabricated `0` out loud has told the user a pour is stuck.

**The peak after a move is re-swept, not adjusted.** Subtracting the moved pour's quantity from the peak is the tempting arithmetic and it is wrong often enough to be dangerous: a pour leaving the peak day lands somewhere else, and what it lands on may already be busy. So a candidate is applied to a copy of the programme — dates, strikes, aggregates and all, so nothing can hand a caller a `lastReleaseAt` from the unmoved version — and the whole sweep runs again. Which is also why every move is costed against **every** catalog id rather than the one that was short: relieving panels by putting two slabs in one week costs props, and a proposal reporting only the id it was asked about would look like a clean win. `raises` is present even when empty, because a cost column with nothing in it reads as free.

**The candidates are two shifts, not every day in the float, and they clear the *other pours* rather than the peak day.** A pour whose plant is held twelve days does not leave an overlap by moving one day past the peak — it stands beside the same pours for eleven of them and the peak has not moved. What takes it clear is starting after the last of them releases or finishing before the first of them arrives. The days proposed are therefore a floor on what is needed rather than the minimum, and that is said: an individual item may come off earlier than the pour's last plant, so a shorter move can sometimes clear that item alone, and searching for the true minimum would be a sweep per candidate day.

**A refusal is the answer, and there are five of them.** `all-pinned`, `nothing-sequenced`, `no-move-helps`, `pour-not-sequenced`, `overlap-unsequenced`. A shortage with no move dropped from the list reads as the tool having nothing to say, so the row is always present and always names which of the five applies — and two of them name a fix the user can act on, since `overlap-unsequenced` is one instruction ("set a cast order on those elements") rather than a dead end. `overlap-unsequenced` is also refused for the *whole* overlap rather than per pour, because an unsequenced pour's float is the programme's entire span and proposing a move off that graph is a free lunch.

**The chat surface's system prompt had to be rewritten rather than extended**, and it is the only surface where that was true. It had been telling the model, in as many words, that "there is no float, no critical path and no answer to 'can this be resequenced to need fewer sets'. Say so rather than working one out." That was correct when written and is now false, and left alone it would have made the model refuse a question the tool beside it can answer — which is worse than the tool not existing, because the refusal is confident.

**None of it is a plan, and every surface says so.** There is no gang, no crew size, no batching plant and no concrete supply in this model, so a move that is feasible against formwork precedence may be impossible for four reasons nothing here can see. It is an argument to take to the planner.

Covered by `sequence.test.ts` (28) and `resequence.test.ts` (12) in core, plus six or seven tests on each of the four surfaces — and the surface tests taught a lesson about test *shape* rather than about sequencing, recorded under Verification above: two of them were written as a conditional over "a move or a stated reason, never neither", both passed, and both had only ever run the refusal half.

## What landed: the gang's time, and why there is no table behind it

**`core/systems/formwork/labour.ts`, on all five surfaces.** Every money figure this feature has produced since the rates shipped has carried the same sentence: *there is no labour in this, and labour is normally the largest of the things outside it.* That sentence was on the panel, in the CSV, in both AI replies and in `cost.excludes`, and repeating it on a fifth surface was never going to be worth as much as answering it. This answers it. `bomLabour(lines, norms)` multiplies the project's own output norms over the same bill the money is counted over and reports the hours beside the money, never inside it.

**There is no shipped norm table, and that is the decision the module exists to record.** It goes further than the one the rates made. A weight has a manufacturer's table behind it and a striking period has a code's; published labour constants also exist — CPWD's Analysis of Rates prints carpenter and mazdoor days per 10 m² of shuttering, Spon's and RSMeans print hours per m² and daily crew outputs — and none of them can be applied here, for two reasons of which the second decides it. They are per m² of a whole trade operation, priced as one item that already contains the backing, the props, the ties and the strike, so applying one to a panel line and again to a waler line charges the same work twice, and applying it once to an area throws away the only quantities this engine actually derived. And **an output norm is a fact about a gang** rather than about a product or a code: the same crew on its tenth identical floor fits panels at twice the rate it managed on its first, and two contractors in one city working from the same price book book different hours against the same wall. There is nothing conservative to fall back to — exactly as with a price, and worse, because a shipped figure would look as authoritative as the weights beside it while being a guess about somebody else's crew.

**A norm is keyed per part kind, not per catalog id**, and the difference is not cosmetic. A gang's output does not distinguish a Framax panel from a TRIO one; forty rows of the same two numbers is thirty-nine that go stale. The consequence caught a test in this session's own suite and is recorded under Verification: a norm against `panel` lands on **every** panel-kind line in the bill, so the hours came out at double what a fixture derived from one catalog id expected. The mistake is available to a user too — a table that looks like a price list is not read like one — which is why the settings note and both AI tool descriptions say the key is a kind in as many words, and the MCP description says the tool will not take one keyed to a product.

**The multiplicand is `BomLine.quantity`, and that is already total fittings.** A project bill is built from every shutter's parts, so a panel type fitted on three pours contributes three times. That is the right figure — a gang is paid to fit a panel each time it fits it — and it is the **opposite** of what `sets.ts` reports, where the peak is what to order. The two must never be crossed: a set count times a norm is the labour of forming the job once. Nothing in the model does cross them, and every surface that prints both says so.

**Erect and strike are separate operations, not a factor.** Striking is faster than erecting on most parts and slower on a few — a tie is a spanner one way and a cut rod, a patched cone and a made-good face the other — and the two fall weeks apart on different halves of a bill: the deck comes off in four days and the props under it stay ten. A single "handling" figure could answer neither, and a strike derived by halving an erect would be a second invented number resting on the first. A project may state one and not the other; that is reported as `erect-only`/`strike-only` and counted for the half it has, rather than filled in or withheld. Withholding it would put a line somebody really did norm into `unnormedFittings` beside the lines nobody touched.

**The hours are reported beside `cost` and never inside it**, which is the discipline the whole change turns on. A hire charge is money to a plant desk for equipment and labour is a gang's time; they are negotiated with different people and move for different reasons — a shorter programme cuts the hire and leaves the hours exactly where they were. So no surface sums them, and the two are pointed at each other instead: `cost.excludes` and the CSV's `Cost basis` cell now say the labour is in the block below and deliberately not in the total, **conditionally** on there being norms at all. A project with no norms reads exactly as it did before, which is why all five pre-existing tests asserting the old exclusion wording passed unchanged.

**Man-hours are not a duration, and that sentence leads the block on every surface.** No gang size exists anywhere in this model, so 400 hours is 400 hours rather than ten days, and dividing by a crew is the reader's decision. It is the first figure in the feature that *looks* like a programme, reads like a duration and is neither, which is why it is stated before the number rather than after it. Three more absences are named rather than approximated: no cleaning, no moving the set between pours, no setting out, no access scaffold, no waiting on concrete, no travel and no supervision; no learning curve, so a job with little repetition is understated and a repetitive one overstated; and no money at all where the project stated norms and no gang rate, which is a complete answer rather than a fault.

**`unnormedFittings` is the figure that says whether the total is an answer or a fragment.** A bill whose panels are normed and whose ties are not produces an hours total that reads complete and is short by every tie in the job. So the count is reported with the kinds **named**, the total is labelled `Man-hours so far` with *a floor, not the work in the job* beside it, and the CSV's own total row carries the same phrase in its label rather than in a footnote a spreadsheet loses. A consumable is refused rather than multiplied for a sharper version of the same reason: applying release agent is real work, and hours-per-fitting has no meaning against a litre, so a line measured in litres gets `not-counted-in-fittings` and no hours.

**The panel's absent state is written at length, which no other section's is.** Where norms exist there is a Labour section; where they do not there is a paragraph naming what is missing and where to record it. Every other optional block in this feature is allowed to be simply absent, because a reader who has recorded no rack does not read the missing hire columns as a claim. Here they would: they have just been told, two lines above, that labour is the largest thing the cost excludes, and a silence under that reads as it having been folded in after all. For the same reason `projectFormworkCaveats` emits the no-norms sentence only where the takeoff has money in it — a takeoff with no cost has already said there is nothing, and two silences about one job read as two problems.

Covered by 16 tests in `labour.test.ts`, 8 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 7 in `solve-project.test.ts`, 3 each in the MCP and chat suites and 2 in `takeoff.test.ts` for the join — and the two fixture bugs behind them are recorded under Verification, because both are mistakes a reader of the output would also make.

## What landed: the date somebody agreed to

**`core/systems/formwork/commitments.ts` — read on the takeoff panel, in the CSV and in both AI replies, written from the assembly inspector and from `commit_pour` on both AI surfaces — and it closes solver phase 10.** Every date in this feature has been stated by whoever typed it, and every module downstream has treated all of them alike. That is the right model for a programme being planned and the wrong one for a programme being built to: at some point a hire company has the delivery in its diary, the following trade has been told when the floor is theirs, and the date has stopped being an opinion. A commitment window is that — **this much of this item is spoken for from here to here, and the pours behind it are ones nobody can quietly move.**

**It is the first module in the feature that needed a schema field, and the reason is the finding rather than an inconvenience.** `sequence.ts` needed none, because precedence was already stated three times over in the scene (`liftIndex`, `castOrder`, `pourId`) and the session before that treated it as a general rule: look harder before adding a field. There was nothing to find here. The scene records what the project **intends** and had nowhere at all to record what anybody has **agreed** — `pourAt` cannot serve, because it answers a different question and answers it for every pour whether or not a booking exists. The absence was the whole of the remaining work in phase 10, and no amount of reading the existing fields would have produced it.

**A commitment is stored as a date, not a boolean, and that is the single decision the module turns on.** `committedPourAt` holds the day that was agreed. A `committed: true` flag beside `pourAt` would have been smaller and would have been wrong in a way nothing downstream could see: move the pour and the flag follows it, so a booking silently re-attaches to a new day as though the hire desk had agreed to it. Two dates that are allowed to disagree is the only shape that can say *the plant is reserved for the 9th and the programme now says the 16th*, which is the expensive state and the one thing no other output in the feature would report.

**The write input is a boolean while the storage is a date, deliberately inverse.** `applyCommitPourPatch({ committed })` takes no date at all and lifts the day off the pour. A caller commits *a pour*, not *a day*, so there is no way to book a day the programme does not carry — and committing an undated pour is refused with the write to make first (`set_pour_date`) rather than accepted as a booking against nothing. One tool with a date in it would let a single call move a pour and book it in the same breath, which is the one combination worth making impossible.

**Moving a committed pour is allowed, and reported.** Sites move booked pours. Refusing the edit would push somebody into clearing the commitment first, which loses the one record that says the plant is still reserved for the old day, so the drift is reported instead: both days, and the **signed** number between them. The sign is the whole of it — later means a set arrives and stands idle at the booked rate, earlier means the pour is due before the plant is, and they are two different phone calls. A third case is distinct again and carries no drift figure at all: the date cleared out from under the booking, so there is no new day to negotiate towards. Every surface keeps the three apart.

**The committed quantity is under the set count's peak on almost every job, and every surface is arranged around not being read as it.** The sweep is `sets.ts`'s own interval sweep restricted to the committed pours, so it is what has been *promised* rather than what the job needs. A reader who takes it for the requirement under-orders by every uncommitted pour, and a reader who sees it beside the peak takes the difference for a shortfall. So it is placed **last** on the panel and last in the CSV, its column is named `Committed` with no "Peak" near it, the first caveat says what it is not before it says what it is, and the AI blocks carry a rule against subtracting the two. There is also no coverage threshold, unlike `sets` — one booked pour is a real booking, where one dated pour out of forty is not a programme.

**The sweep runs over the programme's windows rather than the booked days**, which is the only defensible reading once the two have drifted: the plant is wanted when the pour now happens, and the booking is a promise about a day that has moved. The drift is what says the promise needs renegotiating; sweeping the booked days as well would produce a window nobody is working to.

**The resequencer excludes a committed pour as a candidate and keeps it as an obstacle.** Those are two separate things and both are needed. A booked pour is not a move anybody has left to make, so proposing it is a proposal the reader declines on every read — but it is still standing in the overlap holding its plant, so the shortage it causes is unchanged. The exclusion is per *group*, on any member, because a monolithic pour moves whole and one booked member books the node. Where every pour in an overlap with float is committed, the refusal is `overlap-committed` and it names a fix — release a commitment, or buy the shortage — rather than going quiet. And a proposal that leaves a booked pour out now says which: `committedPourIds` is on every answer, because a move list with a pour missing from it reads as a bug in the sweep.

**It is the one field on `ProjectFormwork` not gated on the scope having any dates**, and the MCP suite is what found that. Clear the last `pourAt` on a job and the schedule, the sets, the acquisition and the sequence all correctly vanish; the plant stays reserved. Gating the commitments with them meant the single state nothing else would report was the state that disappeared, which is recorded under Verification because the 13 unit tests under the module could not have caught it.

**A commitment is a record, not a lock, and that is the last caveat on every surface.** Nothing here stops a pour being moved. It stops the takeoff proposing the move on its own, and reports the disagreement if somebody makes it anyway.

Covered by 13 tests in `commitments.test.ts`, 4 in `schedule-patch.test.ts`, 7 more in `resequence.test.ts`, 4 in `bom-csv.test.ts`, 4 in `solve-project.test.ts`, and 7 each in the MCP and chat suites — and the gate the MCP one found is the substance of the session rather than a bug in a test.

## Shortest path to the next visible win

**Phase 9's cheap class is now empty.** The three clash passes that landed — ties × openings, panels × intersections, ties × waterstops — were exactly the ones whose inputs the solve already carried. The three behind them each need geometry no node has: rebar has no schema at all, the slab below has no capacity, and there is no site boundary. They sit in the permanent `notChecked` list rather than the remaining-work one, and the honest next step for any of them is a schema decision rather than a check.

**The dates have now landed too, and the schema question this section kept asking has an answer** (see "What landed: the day the pour happens"): a date belongs to the **pour**, on the assembly, and the two lead times belong to the project. A 9 m wall in three lifts is three dates a week apart, so there was never a version of this where a field on the wall was right. What that closes is the calendar — delivery, strike and release per pour, a window and an occupancy per scope, on all five surfaces and writable from four. The **sequence** this paragraph named as the thing the dates were most likely to be read as closing has since landed beside them rather than inside them: dependencies and float exist, and nothing computes a date still. A Gantt chart is drawable, a delivery date bookable, and the arrows between the bars are now real.

**The hire cost has now landed too** (see above), and the schema question this section asked has been answered: the rates live on the **project**, as a settings group beside the rack, because a price is a commercial fact about this job rather than a property of a product. What that closes is the *rental*, which was the item with everything downstream of it already built — which parts are hired, how many, and for how long. What it deliberately does not close is the rest of a cost, and what remains is now two items rather than three:

- **Labour has landed** (see "What landed: the gang's time, and why there is no table behind it"), and the prediction this bullet carried was wrong in the way worth recording. It said a norm is "a catalog-adjacent table, not a project one" and that the published constants (CPWD, RSMeans) were the input. Both are false, and the second is why the first is: those constants are per m² of a whole trade operation that already contains the panels, the backing, the ties and the strike, so nothing in this engine's bill of *parts* can consume one without charging the same work several times over. Which forces the norms onto the project for a stronger reason than the rates were — a price is a fact about a supplier and an output is a fact about a **gang**, so there is no authoritative table to be adjacent to. It was right that this is the first *productivity* claim in the feature, and that is exactly why the hours are reported beside the money and never inside it. What that closes is the largest excluded cost in every figure this feature prints. What it does not close is a duration: there is no gang size here, so 400 hours is work rather than time.
- **Transport and craneage** is now the largest thing outside a cost, and it is blocked the same way the gang picks are: it needs `gangs.ts` and a lift weight per set, because a delivery is priced per load and a crane per lift, not per panel.
- **Amortisation / cost-per-use** has been answered in the way that was available and not in the way it was framed. Last session's note said `SetPeak.reuseFactor` made the catalog schema decision avoidable, since a job's own reuse count is a better input than a manufacturer's expected one. That was wrong, and the reason is worth keeping: this job's reuse count says how many times *this job* fits a panel, and amortisation needs how many fittings the panel has *left*. Dividing a list price by the first is an untraceable number wearing a real one's clothes. What landed instead is an internal recharge — owned stock at the project's own hire rate for the span held, outside `totalCost` — which needs no life at all and closes the error that mattered, owning formwork appearing free. What remains genuinely open is amortisation proper, and it is a **catalog schema decision** after all: a life in uses and a residual value per panel type, which is `SheetStock.expectedReuses` widened to `PanelType`. That is a smaller and better-defined piece of work than it was, and it is no longer on the critical path to a usable cost.

**Set counting and the shortage against it have both landed** (see "how many sets the job needs" and "what the job is short of, and whether to own it"), and with them the thing the whole commercial chain was dancing around: the takeoff says what a yard *commits to*, what it does not already have, and what the two courses of getting it cost. Last session's note here predicted the useful sentence would be "you are 40 short and they are used eight times, so buy rather than hire". The reuse count turned out to be the wrong half of that sentence — hire is per unit per month, so uses do not enter the comparison and the span held is the whole of it — and the sentence that shipped is "you are 40 short, they are needed by 1 March, and hiring them costs a fifteenth of buying them, which pays back over 15 jobs like this one".

**The sequence has now landed** (see "What landed: what has to happen before what, and which pour is free to move"), and it answers the question this section named as the loudest gap in the feature: "can this be resequenced to need fewer sets" is a named pour, a signed number of days, the peak the moved programme actually has, and every other catalog id the move makes worse. Both AI surfaces answer it now rather than passing it on. What it did *not* need, and this is the part worth carrying forward, is the scheduler this section assumed: precedence was already in the scene three times over, and float measured against the neighbours' stated dates answers the resource question without inventing a programme. A forward pass would have been more code and a worse claim.

**Phase 10 is closed** (see "What landed: the date somebody agreed to"), and this paragraph's own prediction is worth recording because it was right about the shape and wrong about the size. It said the missing input was not precedence but *agreement*, that the honest next step was a schema decision rather than arithmetic, and that it was the same class of work the pour date was. All three held. What it got wrong is that it called the decision small: the field is one line, and the reason it has to be a **date** rather than the `fixed` flag this paragraph implied took the longest thinking in the change — a flag beside `pourAt` follows it, so a booking silently re-attaches to whatever day the pour moves to, and only two dates allowed to disagree can say the plant is reserved for the 9th while the programme says the 16th. It was also right that this is the thing that lets the feature say a proposal has been *taken*: the resequencer now leaves a committed pour alone and names it rather than re-proposing the same move on every read.

**The next visible win is now the smallest piece of work on this page, and it is the same one as last session with a second half attached.** Nothing yet lets a user *apply* a proposed move: the panel prints "push pour X eight days" and the user goes to the assembly inspector and retypes the date. One button per move, writing the `pourAt` through the same `applyPourDatePatch` both AI surfaces use, closes the loop from "here is the cheaper option" to "the job now does it" — and the second half is that the same row can now offer "and book it", writing `committedPourAt` through `applyCommitPourPatch` in the same click. Two buttons, in that order, is the honest version. It stayed out of this change for the reason it stayed out of the last one — a button that edits the programme from a takeoff panel is a different kind of decision from a figure in one — and there is now nothing left to make trustworthy first.

**Deliberately not built: `solutionCache`.** The plan's schema (§5.1) carries `solutionCache: { hash, solvedAt }` on the assembly, "persisted so a saved scene can produce a BOM without a full re-solve". When an export path needs a BOM without the geometry pass, that is the moment to add it, with the reader and the invalidation in the same change.
